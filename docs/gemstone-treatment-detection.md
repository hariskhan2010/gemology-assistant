## Introduction: Why Treatment Detection Matters

Gemstone treatments are any human-controlled process — other than cutting, polishing, and setting — that alters the appearance, durability, or value of a gemstone. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Jewelry Guides (16 CFR Part 23) require disclosure of any treatment that has a significant effect on a gemstone's value, and of any treatment that creates special care requirements. Similar regulations exist under EU Consumer Protection law, Australia's Competition and Consumer Act, and industry standards enforced by CIBJO (World Jewellery Confederation).

Treatment disclosure matters because:

- **Value implications:** A natural, untreated ruby commands a dramatically higher price than a heavily glass-filled ruby of similar appearance. An untreated emerald is significantly more valuable than an oiled or resin-filled emerald of comparable clarity.
- **Durability and care:** Treated stones may require special care — glass-filled rubies cannot withstand ultrasonic cleaning or acid; oiled emeralds should avoid heat and solvents; irradiated topaz may fade in direct sunlight.
- **Consumer protection:** Regulatory bodies worldwide mandate disclosure; failure to disclose is fraud in most jurisdictions.
- **Market trust:** The gemstone trade relies on accurate disclosure at every point of sale. Laboratories exist to provide independent verification.

### Types of Treatments

Treatments span a continuum from ancient and universally accepted (heating corundum to enhance blue color) to modern and controversial (lead-glass filling of ruby). The key distinction is between:

- **Traditional enhancement:** Heat treatment of corundum, oiling of emeralds, bleaching of pearls — long practiced, widely accepted when disclosed.
- **Modern treatment:** HPHT of diamond, beryllium diffusion of sapphire, polymer impregnation of jadeite — requiring advanced analytical methods for detection.
- **Fraudulent treatment:** Heavy glass filling, dyeing to simulate a different species, coating to disguise origin — usually detectable with standard tools and requiring explicit disclosure.

### Detection Philosophy

Treatment detection follows a systematic elimination process:

1. **Standard gemological examination** (loupe, microscope, refractometer, polariscope, UV lamp, spectroscope) — catches the majority of treatments.
2. **Intermediate techniques** (Chelsea filter, immersion microscopy, hot point testing, acetone wipe) — resolves ambiguous cases.
3. **Advanced instrumentation** (FTIR, Raman, ED-XRF, LA-ICP-MS, PL spectroscopy, DiamondView) — definitive identification for resistant treatments and forensic documentation.

## 1. Heat Treatment

Heat treatment is the oldest and most widely practiced gemstone enhancement. By heating stones to 400–1900°C (often with controlled atmospheres), color can be improved, lightened, darkened, or changed entirely. It is considered an accepted trade practice for many species.

### Affected Species

- **Corundum** (ruby, sapphire — most common, heated at 1000–1900°C)
- **Tanzanite** (brown vanadium-rich zoisite → violet-blue via heating at 400–650°C — nearly ALL tanzanite on market is heat-treated)
- **Zircon** (brown → blue, colorless, golden via heating at 900–1000°C in reducing atmosphere)
- **Amethyst** (violet → citrine yellow/golden via heating at ~500°C)
- **Aquamarine** (greenish-blue → pure blue via heating at 400–500°C)
- **Topaz** (pink/brown imperial topaz color enhancement via heating; brown → colorless)
- **Tourmaline** (dark green → lighter green; dark red/pink → lighter; some color changes)
- **Quartz** (various color changes: amethyst to citrine, smoky to colorless)
- **Morganite** (salmon-pink to deeper pink via heating)
- **Sapphire** (geuda sapphire from Sri Lanka → blue via heating; colorless → blue via heating with Be diffusion in some cases)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–70x)

**Inclusions as thermal history recorders:**

- **Dissolved silk in corundum:** Needle-like rutile inclusions (silk) begin to dissolve at ~1300°C. Partially dissolved silk appears as broken, segmented, or "fuzzy" needles. Complete dissolution leaves clear, inclusion-free zones. The degree of dissolution correlates with temperature and duration.
- **Discoid halos / "fried" inclusions:** Mineral inclusions (zircon, apatite, etc.) surrounded by tension halos — circular or oval fractures caused by differential thermal expansion. Common in heated corundum. Natural (unheated) corundum may also show halos if the inclusion is radioactive, but heated halos are typically more abundant and uniform.
- **Diffused blue color zones in sapphire:** Heated sapphire often shows characteristic "bleeding" of blue color from former color band boundaries. Color tends to be more diffuse and smudged compared to sharp, distinct color zoning in unheated stones.
- **"Fried" or cloudy appearance:** Overheating (above 1700°C for corundum) can create a cloudy, milky, or "sugary" appearance due to incipient melting, exsolution, or glass formation along fractures.
- **Partial healing of fluid inclusions:** Primary fluid inclusions (liquid + gas bubbles) may show evidence of decrepitation or healing — creating a "sponge-like" or "fingerprint" texture of tiny secondary inclusions.
- **Altered mineral inclusions:** Hematite inclusions in corundum convert to magnetite at high temperature (detectable by Raman or magnetic susceptibility). Pyrite inclusions decompose.
- **Color zoning changes:** Sharp, angular color zones become diffuse. In some cases, heat can create new color zoning from element diffusion.
- **Surface alterations:** Heated stones may show "orange peel" texture on facet surfaces when overheated, visible under darkfield illumination at 40–70x.

**Specific observations by species:**

- **Heated tanzanite:** No diagnostic inclusion features specific to heat — natural tanzanite is nearly always brown and heat-treated to blue/violet. The presence of brown pleochroic shades in a blue tanzanite indicates lower-temperature heat or partial treatment.
- **Heated zircon:** Resorption features, rounded crystal outlines, altered inclusions. Heating reduces SG and may cause metamict zircons to recrystallize partially.
- **Heated amethyst → citrine:** No diagnostic inclusions — essentially all citrine is heated amethyst. Look for "Brazil law twinning" (polysynthetic twinning in amethyst) which may persist after heating, confirming amethyst origin.
- **Heated aquamarine:** Greenish beryl heated to blue shows no distinct inclusion features. The treatment is detected primarily by the absence of green pleochroism.

### Spectroscopic Detection

- **UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy:** Heated corundum shows loss of certain Fe-related absorption bands — especially the 450 nm, 377 nm, and 388 nm bands (Fe³⁺) may weaken. In heated sapphire, the Fe²⁺–Ti⁴⁺ intervalence charge transfer band at ~580 nm may intensify or shift. In heated amethyst, the 545 nm band (Fe⁴⁺ color center) disappears as color is removed.
- **FTIR spectroscopy:** The OH stretching region (3000–3700 cm⁻¹) in corundum is diagnostic:
  - **Unheated ruby/sapphire:** Sharp peaks at 3309 cm⁻¹ and 3373 cm⁻¹ (boehmite inclusions or O–H stretching).
  - **Heated corundum:** Loss or significant reduction of the 3309 cm⁻¹ peak. A 3185 cm⁻¹ peak may appear in some heated stones. Complete absence of OH peaks suggests very high-temperature heating.
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Phase changes in mineral inclusions — hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) → magnetite (Fe₃O₄); goethite → hematite; pyrite → pyrrhotite. Raman can identify inclusion species that have undergone thermal alteration.
- **Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy:** In corundum, the Cr³⁺ R-line ratio and linewidth can be affected by heat. Unheated ruby typically shows sharper R-lines.

### Advanced Detection

- **LA-ICP-MS (Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry):** Trace element profiles (Mg, Ti, V, Cr, Fe, Ga) are used to establish geographic origin. Heating does not significantly alter trace element concentrations, so origin determination is still possible. However, heating can redistribute elements within the crystal. The Ti concentration in corundum decreases with heating as rutile dissolves into the lattice.
- **XRF (X-Ray Fluorescence):** Elemental fingerprint showing major and minor elements. Not directly diagnostic for heat treatment, but can rule out other treatments (e.g., by detecting surface contamination or filling materials).
- **EPR (Electron Paramagnetic Resonance):** Can detect Fe³⁺ and Cr³⁺ site occupancy changes induced by heat. Useful in research contexts.
- **Synchrotron X-ray microtomography:** 3D visualization of inclusion behavior during heating — research tool.

### Reliability & Limitations

- Heat treatment detection confidence ranges from **obvious** (extensively dissolved silk + discoid halos) to **impossible** (low-temperature heat leaves zero diagnostic features).
- Subtle or low-temperature heating (below ~900°C for corundum) may not alter inclusions sufficiently for confident detection.
- Some unheated corundum naturally lacks silk or contains discoid halos from radioactive inclusions — these can be misinterpreted.
- **Blanket statement:** Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A stone showing no diagnostic features of heat may still be heated.
- Heated stones are widely accepted in the trade when disclosed. The primary concern is detection for accurate grading and valuation.

## 2. Fracture Filling / Glass Filling

Fracture filling involves infusing a glassy substance (usually lead-silicate glass) into surface-reaching fractures to reduce their visibility. Glass-filled ruby is the most significant example — a controversial treatment that creates a composite material with dramatically reduced durability.

### Affected Species

- **Ruby** (lead glass filling — most common and commercially significant — typically low-quality corundum from Mong Hsu, Myanmar)
- **Sapphire** (less common, but glass-filled blue and fancy sapphires exist)
- **Diamond** (fracture filling with high-RI glass to mask feathers — less common since ~2000 because of insurance/durability concerns)
- **Emerald** (glass filling of surface-reaching fractures — rare, usually oil or resin preferred)
- **Turquoise** (plastic or epoxy fillers — overlaps with polymer impregnation)
- **Spinel** (glass-filled fracture treatments reported)
- **Quartz** (occasional glass filling of large fractures)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–70x)

- **Flash effect — PATHOGNOMONIC for glass-filled ruby:** When rotating the stone under a fiber-optic light, the filler produces iridescent color flashes — typically electric blue, orange, green, or violet — along filled fractures. This is the single most reliable diagnostic feature.
- **Gas bubbles within filler:** Rounded, spherical gas bubbles trapped in the glass during manufacture. Size varies from microscopic to just visible at 10x. Multiple bubbles along a fracture path are diagnostic.
- **Devitrification zones:** Crystallization of the glass filler over time, producing a sugary or granular texture along the fracture. Indicates older or poorly made filler.
- **"Lakes" of filler at fracture openings:** Where the filler meets the surface, it may pool or form concave menisci. Under reflected light, these lakes appear as smooth, glassy areas contrasting with the stone's surface.
- **Crackled network:** The filled fracture may appear as a crackled, branching network with lower relief than natural fractures. The filler fills the entire void, making the fracture less visible in some orientations.
- **Color concentration along fractures:** In glass-filled ruby, the red/pink color is often more saturated along the filled fractures because of light interaction with the filler.
- **Low-relief vs. high-relief natural surface:** The filler typically has a lower relief at the surface — it may appear slightly depressed due to shrinkage during cooling.
- **Luster differences:** Under reflected light, the filler has a vitreous (glassy) luster vs. the slightly adamantine luster of corundum.
- **"Orange peel" surface:** In heavily filled stones, the surface may show an orange-peel texture from shrinkage of the glass layer.

### Advanced Detection

- **ED-XRF / SEM-EDS (Energy Dispersive XRF / Scanning Electron Microscopy with EDS):** Detects heavy elements in the filler:
  - **Lead (Pb):** Most common — lead-silicate glass (PbO content 50–80%).
  - **Bismuth (Bi):** Found in some modern fillers.
  - **Other elements:** Silicon, aluminum, calcium, barium, strontium — depending on filler composition.
  - XRF spot analysis at the fracture site vs. an unfractured area shows distinctly different elemental profiles.
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Identifies glass composition — broad bands characteristic of amorphous SiO₂-PbO glass at ~400–600 cm⁻¹ and ~950–1100 cm⁻¹. Devitrified zones may show crystalline peaks (e.g., PbSiO₃ or Pb₂SiO₄).
- **UV-Vis spectroscopy:** Broad absorption from lead glass may alter the transmission spectrum, but is not definitive on its own.
- **Micro-CT (Computed Tomography):** 3D mapping of filler distribution — useful for quantifying the extent of treatment.

### Durability Concerns

- **Thermal shock:** Glass-filled ruby can crack or craze from the heat of a jeweler's torch or hot water. The glass filler has a different thermal expansion coefficient than corundum.
- **Ultrasonic damage:** Ultrasonic cleaning can cause filler to dislodge, crack, or fall out. **DO NOT ultrasonic clean glass-filled stones.**
- **Acid damage:** Hydrochloric, hydrofluoric, and sulfuric acids can attack the lead-silicate glass. Even mild acid (citric, phosphoric) may etch the filler over time.
- **Abrasion:** The filler is much softer (Mohs ~5–6) than corundum (Mohs 9). Facet edges over filled fractures wear down quickly in a ring setting.
- **Heat from repolishing:** Recutting or repolishing can damage or expose filled areas.

### Reliability & Limitations

- Flash effect is diagnostic — if you see it, the stone is filled.
- Very small fractures with minimal filler may not produce a visible flash effect, requiring close inspection at 40–70x with a bright fiber-optic light.
- Older fillers (pre-2000) may have devitrified significantly, reducing or eliminating the flash effect.
- Glass-filled ruby that has been recut may have exposed original fractures at the surface — these areas may show no filler, making detection harder.

## 3. Oiling / Resin Impregnation

Oiling is the traditional method for reducing the visibility of surface-reaching fractures in emerald. Natural oils (cedarwood oil) or synthetic resins (Opticon, Permasafe, ExCel, palm oil) are impregnated under vacuum or pressure into the stone's fissures.

### Affected Species

- **Emerald** (most common — cedarwood oil, Opticon, Permasafe, ExCel, various polymer resins)
- **Ruby** (fractures oiled to improve clarity — less common)
- **Sapphire** (similar oiling of fractures)
- **Turquoise** (oiling as a temporary stabilization — rare)
- **Jadeite** (oiling as a less-common alternative to polymer impregnation)

### Types of Fillers Used in Emerald

| Filler Type | Examples | RI | UV Fluorescence | FTIR Signature | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Natural oils | Cedarwood oil | ~1.510 | Weak yellow-green | C-H stretch ~2925, 2854 cm⁻¹ | Evaporates over months |
| Synthetic resins | Opticon | ~1.520 | Strong yellow-green | C-H stretch + aromatic peaks | Relatively stable |
| Modern polymers | Permasafe, ExCel | ~1.55–1.56 | Bright whitish-yellow | Distinct FTIR fingerprint | Very stable |
| Mixed | Palm oil + resin blends | Variable | Variable | Complex | Variable |

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–50x)

- **UV fluorescence — KEY detection method:** Under long-wave UV (365 nm), oil and resin in fractures typically fluoresce yellow-green, whitish-yellow, or greenish-blue. Natural emerald shows inert to weak red fluorescence (Cr³⁺). The contrast between fluorescent-filled fractures and inert host is diagnostic.
  - Cedarwood oil: Moderate yellow-green fluorescence.
  - Opticon: Strong yellow-green fluorescence.
  - Permasafe/ExCel: Bright whitish-yellow fluorescence.
  - Hardening/dried resins: May show weaker or different fluorescence.
- **Dried oil residue at surface:** "Lakes" or "craters" of dried oil at fracture openings. Under reflected light, these appear as colorless to yellowish deposits with a waxy luster.
- **"Feathering" pattern:** Partially dried oil along a fracture creates a feather-like or dendritic pattern — oil that has begun to evaporate and congeal.
- **Color concentration along fractures:** The oil itself may be colored (tinted green in some emerald treatments), creating darker green zones along filled fractures.
- **Interference colors:** Thin oil films in partially filled fractures may produce rainbow interference colors under reflected light.
- **Hot needle test (destructive, rarely used):** Touching a hot needle to a filled fracture causes oil to bubble and produce a characteristic smell (burnt wood for cedar oil, plastic/resin for synthetics). DESTRUCTIVE — only use when explicitly authorized.
- **Acetone test (non-destructive):** Rubbing a suspected oiled area with acetone on a cotton swab may dissolve surface oil. The swab can then be examined for residue under UV light. Non-destructive but may remove surface oil from the stone.
- **Dehydration/rehydration test:** Oil-filled emeralds left in dry heat (lamp) may show "sweating" — oil migrating to the surface as it expands. An old method, less used today.

### Advanced Detection

- **FTIR spectroscopy (DESKTOP):** The most definitive non-destructive method for identifying filler type:
  - **Cedarwood oil:** C-H stretching peaks at ~2925 cm⁻¹ and ~2854 cm⁻¹ (aliphatic CH₂). Sharp but simple spectrum.
  - **Opticon:** Additional aromatic C-H stretch at ~3030 cm⁻¹ and C=C stretching at ~1600 cm⁻¹. Distinct fingerprint region.
  - **Permasafe:** Strong absorption at ~2870, 2930, 2960 cm⁻¹ (CH₃ and CH₂ stretching of polymer). Also shows C=O peak at ~1735 cm⁻¹.
  - **ExCel:** Very similar to Permasafe but with additional peaks in the fingerprint region.
  - **No filler (natural emerald):** OH stretching bands from water in beryl channels (~3400–3700 cm⁻¹) and possibly weak CO₂ bands (~2350 cm⁻¹).
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Can identify specific resins in situ within fractures:
  - Opticon: Strong fluorescence background with characteristic peaks at ~1000, ~1600 cm⁻¹ (aromatic ring breathing).
  - Permasafe/ExCel: Strong peaks at ~1450, ~1730 cm⁻¹ (CH₂ bending, C=O stretch).
- **UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy:** Not directly diagnostic for oil/resin, but can confirm the natural color origin (Cr³⁺ and V³⁺ absorption in emerald).
- **Micro-chemical analysis:** GC-MS (Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry) on extracted filler — destructive, definitive, used only in research.

### Detection Difficulty & Limitations

- **Heavy oiling/resin:** Easy to detect with UV fluorescence and microscopy.
- **Light oiling:** Very difficult — may require desktop FTIR with careful positioning on the filled fracture. Surface residue may be minimal.
- **Old, dried oil:** May fluoresce very weakly or not at all. The oil has evaporated or polymerized, leaving little detectable residue. FTIR may still detect residual organic material.
- **"New" oiling treatments:** Some modern treatments use oils with very low fluorescence, deliberately making UV detection harder.
- **Emerald in jewelry:** Setting can make fracture access difficult; oil may have migrated or been partially removed during setting.

## 4. Irradiation

Irradiation uses high-energy particles or photons (gamma rays, neutrons, electrons, protons) to create color centers in gemstones by displacing electrons or creating crystal lattice defects. The resulting color depends on the type of irradiation, dose, and the stone's trace element chemistry.

### Affected Species

- **Topaz** (blue — most commercially significant irradiation treatment: colorless → Sky Blue, Swiss Blue, London Blue)
- **Diamond** (green, blue-green, pink, red, purple, blue, black — via electron, neutron, or proton irradiation)
- **Quartz** (amethyst, smoky quartz, citrine — natural color often also from irradiation, but commercial irradiation is common)
- **Tourmaline** (color enhancement — pink/red intensified, some green colors)
- **Spodumene** (kunzite — pink intensified but very unstable; hiddenite — green enhanced)
- **Beryl** (maxixe-type — colorless/pink → deep blue; VERY unstable, fades rapidly in light)
- **Spinel** (color enhancement — various colors)
- **Pearl** (silver/blue/gray body color — gamma irradiation of freshwater and saltwater pearls)
- **Tektites** (color enhancement)
- **Chalcedony** (color enhancement)

### Color Changes Produced

| Species | Starting Color | Final Color | Stability | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topaz | Colorless, pale blue | Sky Blue, Swiss Blue, London Blue | Stable | Most common gem irradiation treatment |
| Topaz | Brown/yellow | Pink (rare) | Stable | Requires heating after irradiation |
| Diamond | Brown | Green, blue-green, pink | Stable | Requires annealing after irradiation |
| Diamond | Colorless | Black (radiation-induced graphitization) | Stable | Heavy-dose treatment |
| Quartz | Colorless | Amethyst (purple) | Stable in dark, fades in light | Natural amethyst forms the same way |
| Quartz | Colorless | Smoky (brown-gray) | Very stable | Natural smoky is the same color center |
| Kunzite | Pale pink | Intense pink | **Unstable** — fades in days to weeks in sunlight | Requires dark storage |
| Maxixe beryl | Pink | Deep blue | **Unstable** — fades rapidly | Rarely seen today |
| Pearl | White/cream | Silver, gray, blue-gray, black | Moderately stable | May fade in strong light |

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–45x)

- **Color zoning in irradiated topaz:** Color is often unevenly distributed, with darker concentration near the pavilion tip (culet) and lighter color near the crown. This is because the irradiation source is typically directed from below.
- **"Umbrella effect" in topaz:** Darker blue at the pavilion tip, fading toward the girdle — caused by the geometry of irradiation exposure. The pavilion tip receives the highest dose.
- **Colorless girdle in blue topaz:** The girdle area may remain colorless or pale because it was shielded during irradiation.
- **Absence of natural color causes:** Blue topaz from irradiation shows no iron-related absorption features in the spectroscope — natural blue sapphire or blue zircon would show Fe bands. This is a key screening test.
- **Color stability test:** Expose a small, inconspicuous area to strong UV light (or sunlight) for several hours. Kunzite and maxixe-beryl will show rapid fading. Irradiated topaz and diamond are stable.
- **Surface damage at high doses:** Very high-dose irradiation can cause surface pitting, discoloration, or even structural damage to the stone.
- **Annealing evidence:** Some irradiated stones are subsequently heated (annealed) to modify the color — look for both irradiation and heat indicators.

### Spectroscopic Detection

- **UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy (CRITICAL for irradiated diamonds):**
  - **Irradiated diamond:** Characteristic GR1 (General Radiation 1) absorption band at 741 nm (zero-phonon line) — a broad band in the red-IR region that gives green color. Also: ND1 (393 nm) and H2, H3 centers after annealing.
  - **Irradiated topaz:** The blue color is caused by a broad absorption band centered at ~620–630 nm (O⁻ hole center). No absorption from Fe²⁺ or Ti⁴⁺. The shape and position of the band differs from any natural blue color origin.
  - **Irradiated quartz (smoky):** Broad absorption from ~400–700 nm with a peak at ~470 nm (Al-hole center).
  - **Irradiated quartz (amethyst):** Absorption band at ~545 nm (Fe⁴⁺ center).
- **PL spectroscopy (Photoluminescence):** In irradiated diamond, PL detects NV⁰ and NV⁻ centers (zero-phonon lines at 575 nm and 637 nm), H3 center (503 nm), H4 center (496 nm), and GR1 (741 nm). These color centers are induced or enhanced by irradiation.
- **EPR (Electron Paramagnetic Resonance) — DEFINITIVE for irradiation detection:** Detects unpaired electrons trapped at lattice defects (color centers). Each color center type has a characteristic EPR signature. This is the most definitive non-destructive method for confirming irradiation.
- **FTIR spectroscopy:** In topaz, the OH stretching region may show changes after irradiation, but this is rarely diagnostic on its own.

### Advanced Detection

- **PL mapping (hyperspectral):** High-resolution PL imaging maps the distribution of color centers across the stone. Irradiation-induced centers show characteristic geometric patterns corresponding to the irradiation source direction.
- **Cathodoluminescence (CL) imaging:** Reveals growth zoning and defect distributions that differ between natural and irradiated color origins.
- **Synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES/EXAFS):** Probes the local atomic environment of color-causing defects — research tool.

### Disclosure Requirements

- Irradiated topaz and diamond **must be disclosed** in most jurisdictions.
- **Safety note:** Blue topaz is safe for handling and wearing — residual radioactivity decays to background levels within months of treatment (short half-life isotopes: ~2.7 months for Co⁶⁰, days for others). All commercially irradiated topaz is tested and certified as safe before distribution.
- Consumer disclosure is still required even though the stone is safe — the treatment affects value and perception.
- Irradiated diamond can mimic natural colored diamond — PL spectroscopy is often the only way to differentiate definitively.

## 5. Dyeing

Dyeing introduces colored organic or inorganic compounds into porous stones or along fractures to impart or deepen color. It is one of the oldest gemstone treatments and among the most detectable.

### Affected Species

- **Jadeite** (lavender, green — most commercially significant dyed stone; often dyed to simulate "imperial green")
- **Pearl** (black, pink, gold, blue — dye penetrates the nacre)
- **Turquoise** (blue, green — dye to improve or even color)
- **Lapis Lazuli** (blue — dye to deepen or even color)
- **Coral** (red, pink, black — dye to intensify or change color)
- **Agate** (banded — vividly colored dyed agate is common in the market)
- **Chalcedony** (blue — dyed to simulate "blue lace agate" or other varieties)
- **Howlite** (dyed blue to resemble turquoise — VERY common imitation)
- **Quartz** (rock crystal dyed — less common, usually visible in fractures)
- **Opal** (body stain — sugar-acid treatment to darken body color; see section 12)
- **Emerald** (green dye in fractures — very rare, usually a sign of fraud)
- **Ruby** (pink dye — rare, usually on low-grade material)
- **Magna sit down** (dyed quartzite — common turquoise imitation)
- **Marble** (dyed to resemble various gemstones)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–45x, preferably immersion)

- **Color concentration in fractures and surface — KEY diagnostic:** Under magnification, dyed stones show color concentrated along fractures, grain boundaries, and surface-reaching fissures. The dye follows these pathways like a road map.
- **Uneven color distribution:** Dye rarely produces a perfectly uniform color. Look for blotchy, patchy, or streaky color distribution. In agates, the dye often fills specific bands unevenly.
- **Dye traces in surface pits and facet junctions:** Color may pool at facet junctions, around culet, or in surface pits.
- **Fingernail scratches reveal undyed interior:** A scratch across a facet may show white or colorless material underneath the colored surface. In jadeite, dyed lavender often shows white interior at scratches or chipped areas.
- **Acetone wipe test (non-destructive):** Rub a white cotton swab moistened with acetone against the stone. If the dye is surface-concentrated, it may transfer to the swab. **Not all dyes are acetone-soluble** — some are alcohol-soluble, others are insoluble in common solvents.
- **UV fluorescence:** Dyes often fluoresce unnaturally — unusually strong, patchy, or in colors not seen in natural stones. Dyed jadeite (lavender) typically shows strong orange fluorescence under LWUV — natural lavender jadeite is usually inert.
- **Chelsea color filter reaction:** Dye may show a different Chelsea filter reaction than the natural stone. For example, dyed jadeite appears pinkish under the Chelsea filter (simulating the reaction of natural chromium-colored jadeite) — not diagnostic alone.
- **Immersion microscopy:** Viewing the stone immersed in a liquid with similar RI (e.g., benzyl benzoate for jadeite) makes the dye concentration in fractures even more visible.
- **Crossed polarizers:** In dyed agates and chalcedonies, the dye follows the fibrous/cryptocrystalline structure grain boundaries.

### Advanced Detection

- **Raman spectroscopy:** Identifies organic dye molecules by their characteristic Raman fingerprint. For example, aniline-based dyes, azo dyes, and natural dye extracts each have distinct spectra. Can also identify the host mineral simultaneously.
- **UV-Vis spectroscopy:** Dye absorption bands are often distinct from natural chromophore bands. For example, dyed green jadeite shows Cr³⁺ absorption bands (similar to natural) but may also show additional organic dye bands. Dyed lavender jadeite shows Mn²⁺ bands (if natural) or broad organic dye absorption.
- **FTIR spectroscopy:** Organic dyes produce C-H, C=O, N-H, and C-N stretching bands in the mid-IR region (4000–400 cm⁻¹). May be masked by the host mineral's absorption, but surface-sensitive ATR-FTIR can pick up surface dye.
- **Dye extraction + GC-MS (destructive):** For forensic or research purposes, dye can be extracted with a suitable solvent and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Destructive.
- **X-ray fluorescence (XRF):** Not directly useful for organic dyes, but can detect metal-complex dyes (e.g., chromium-based dyes on jadeite) — elevated Cr in a stone that should not naturally contain Cr.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Obvious cases:** Color in fractures visible at 10x, acetone pickup, patchy color — easy.
- **Subtle cases:** Modern dyes with good penetration and similar color to the natural stone may be very difficult. Dyed lapis lazuli with deep blue dye that has been waxed can look natural.
- **Dyed agate:** So pervasive in the market that detection is rarely needed — most "blue agate" on the market is dyed, and this is generally known and accepted.
- **Dyed jadeite:** B-jade (polymer-impregnated) sometimes also contains dye — this is a combination treatment.

## 6. Surface Diffusion / Coating

Surface treatments create a thin colored layer on the stone's surface to simulate a more valuable color or to add a desirable optical effect. These treatments differ fundamentally from lattice diffusion (where elements penetrate the crystal lattice — see Section 11).

### Surface Diffusion (Corundum)

Diffusion treatment applies color-producing elements (Fe, Ti, Cr, V, Be) to the surface of corundum and heats it to high temperature (~1600–1800°C), causing the elements to diffuse into a thin layer (0.1–1.0 mm deep).

#### Affected Species
- **Sapphire** (Padparadscha, yellow, orange, blue mimicry)
- **Ruby** (surface diffusion of Cr — rare, usually lattice diffusion for Cr)
- **Star sapphire/ruby** (Ti diffusion to create or enhance asterism)

#### Detection
- **Immersion microscopy — KEY test:** The color is concentrated **at the surface only**. Submerge the stone in a liquid with matching RI (methylene iodide, RI ~1.74 for corundum) and examine the girdle and facet junctions. The color appears as a thin skin, often pooling at facet edges.
- **Color "pooling" in scratches and surface pits:** Surface scratches and polishing pits will be colored, showing that the color is only skin-deep. In immersion, these colored scratches are clearly visible against the colorless interior.
- **Abraded facet edges:** Facet edges that have been abraded or worn during setting appear lighter or colorless where the diffused layer has been removed.
- **Girdle coloration:** The girdle often shows the most intense color concentration because it receives the most diffusing elements during the process.
- **Color unevenness:** The diffused layer may be uneven in thickness, resulting in patchy color distribution across the stone.
- **Refractometer:** RI measurement is **NOT diagnostic** — the diffused layer is too thin to affect the RI reading.

#### Advanced Detection
- **ED-XRF:** Surface analysis shows elevated concentrations of diffused elements (Fe, Ti, Cr, V) compared to the interior. Spot analysis at different locations can map concentration variations.
- **LA-ICP-MS (depth profiling):** The definitive method. Laser ablation progressively sampling from surface to interior shows an exponential decrease in the diffused element concentration — characteristic of a diffusion profile. Unheated or naturally heated stones show constant trace element concentrations from surface to interior.
- **SEM-EDS:** Scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy on the surface shows the diffused element layer. Cross-sectioning (destructive) shows the layer thickness directly.

### Coating

Coating applies a thin film of colored material (metal oxides, colored lacquers, or interference films) to the surface of a gemstone.

#### Affected Species
- **Tanzanite** (coated blue to deepen color — common in the 1990s–2000s; coated tanzanite looks unnatural, too deeply blue)
- **Topaz** (coated with metal oxides to produce "mystic topaz" — iridescent rainbow colors)
- **Quartz** (coated — similar rainbow coatings)
- **Cubic Zirconia** (coated with colored films)
- **Pearl** (coated with nacre-like substances or iridescent films)
- **Diamond** (very thin film coatings to modify color or improve color grade — rare, considered fraudulent)
- **Labradorite** (coated to enhance labradorescence — uncommon)

#### Materials Used
- Metal oxides: TiO₂ (titanium dioxide), Al₂O₃, SiO₂, ZrO₂
- Thin-film interference coatings: Multiple layers of dielectric materials
- Colored lacquers: Organic polymer-based coatings
- Metal films: Gold, silver, titanium (on some costume jewelry stones)

#### Detection
- **Reflected light microscopy — BEST method:** Examine the surface under strong reflected light at 20–70x:
  - **Interference films:** Rainbow-colored reflections, characteristic of thin-film interference.
  - **Scratches in coating:** Scratches that penetrate only the coating reveal the stone's true color underneath.
  - **Partially worn coating at facet edges:** Coating is thinnest at facet edges and wears first. Look for lighter or colorless facet edges.
  - **Peeling/flaking:** Coating may lift at the edges, especially on curved surfaces.
  - **Texture:** Coated surfaces may show a slightly different texture or luster than natural facets.
- **UV-Vis reflection spectroscopy:** Produces interference patterns specific to the coating thickness and refractive index. A series of maxima and minima in the reflection spectrum indicates a thin-film coating.
- **Scratch test with scalpel (if needed, destructive):** Gently scratching the surface at the girdle with a scalpel — coating flakes off, revealing uncolored material underneath. **Only with owner permission.**
- **Acetone test:** Some coatings (especially lacquer-type) dissolve in acetone. Dampen a cotton swab and rub the surface gently.
- **ED-XRF:** Surface analysis detects elements present in the coating (Ti, Al, Si, Zr, etc.) that are not part of the host stone's composition.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Obvious:** Worn coating at facet edges, scratches revealing underlying color, unnatural appearance.
- **Subtle:** Modern vacuum-deposited oxide coatings can be very thin, evenly applied, and highly durable — requiring SEM or advanced surface analysis for detection.
- **"Mystic topaz":** So widely known that its coating is part of the marketing name — detection is more about confirming the coating type than discovering it.

## 7. HPHT (High Pressure High Temperature)

HPHT treatment subjects diamond (and occasionally corundum) to extremely high pressures (50–70 kbar) and temperatures (1300–2000°C) simultaneously to modify color or improve clarity. Originally developed for diamond synthesis, it was adapted for treatment in the late 1990s.

### Affected Species

- **Diamond** (by far the most common — color improvement and type modification)
- **Corundum** (less common — HPHT used to dissolve silk and improve clarity in some cases)

### Diamond HPHT Treatment Types

| Starting Diamond Type | Starting Color | Final Color | Mechanism | Commercial Significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Type IIa (low N) | Brown | Near-colorless (D–H) | Plastic deformation annealing — removes brown color centers | **Most common HPHT treatment** |
| Type Ia (aggregated N) | Brown | Fancy yellow, green-yellow, orangey | Nitrogen aggregation state changes | Common |
| Type Ia (low N) | Brown | Near-colorless (less successful than IIa) | Partial nitrogen aggregation | Moderate |
| Type IIb (B-doped) | Brown | Blue | Boron activation; brown removed | Rare — produces blue diamonds |
| All types | Various | Fancy pink, red, purple | Plastic deformation + annealing + irradiation in multi-step processes | Rare, high-value |

### Detection: Standard Gemological Tools

- **Crossed polarizers (polariscope) — KEY screening tool:**
  - Natural untreated diamonds typically show strong birefringence strain patterns ("tatami" or cross-hatched patterns) due to natural plastic deformation.
  - **HPHT-treated diamonds show much weaker strain or no strain (black extinction through the entire stone).**
  - HPHT synthetic diamonds similarly show no strain — this test is a strong indicator but not definitive (some natural diamonds have weak strain).
  - **Caveat:** Type IIa natural diamonds may also show very weak strain, so the polariscope test is screening, not conclusive.
- **UV fluorescence imaging:**
  - Under LWUV (365 nm), HPHT-treated diamonds often show **greenish-yellow fluorescence** — distinct from the typical blue fluorescence of natural diamonds.
  - Not all HPHT-treated diamonds show this, and some natural diamonds can also show greenish fluorescence.
- **Diamond Type by FTIR:**
  - HPHT treatment is most commonly applied to Type IIa diamonds (no measurable nitrogen). Finding a near-colorless Type IIa diamond should raise suspicion.
  - However, natural Type IIa diamonds exist (the Cullinan, Koh-i-Noor, etc.) — a Type IIa classification alone is not diagnostic.

### Definitive Detection Methods

- **DiamondView (UV fluorescence imaging at high magnification):**
  - **THE most reliable method** for detecting HPHT treatment in diamond.
  - **HPHT-treated diamonds** show characteristic geometric growth patterns — octahedral and cubic sector zoning with sharp boundaries between sectors.
  - **Natural diamonds** show irregular, non-geometric growth patterns (e.g., octahedral zoning, irregular patches).
  - **HPHT synthetic diamonds** show even more pronounced geometric patterns (square or octahedral sector zoning, sometimes a cross-shaped pattern).
  - Imaging at various wavelengths (UV, blue, green) provides complementary information.
- **PL spectroscopy (Photoluminescence at liquid nitrogen temperature):**
  - **H3 center (503 nm):** HPHT treatment creates or enhances H3 centers (nitrogen-vacancy pairs). Strong H3 emission with no H4 is consistent with HPHT treatment of Type Ia diamonds.
  - **H4 center (496 nm):** Often present in natural diamonds but may be modified by HPHT.
  - **NV⁰ (575 nm) and NV⁻ (637 nm):** HPHT treatment can anneal irradiation-induced NV centers.
  - **N3 center (415 nm):** May be reduced by HPHT treatment.
  - **GR1 (741 nm):** Absent in HPHT-only treatment (present only if irradiated before HPHT).
- **FTIR spectroscopy:**
  - **Nitrogen aggregation:** HPHT treatment at high temperature can cause nitrogen aggregation states to change. Type IaA (paired N) can convert to Type IaB (aggregated N) — the B' peak at ~1175 cm⁻¹ may be present.
  - In some HPHT treatments, nitrogen aggregation may be incomplete, showing a mix of A and B centers.
  - **Platelet peaks (1360–1370 cm⁻¹):** HPHT treatment can modify or eliminate platelet peaks.
- **UV-Vis spectroscopy:**
  - The "brown color center" in diamond (which HPHT removes) is a broad, featureless absorption increasing toward the UV. After HPHT, this absorption is removed, producing a flatter spectrum.
  - HPHT yellow diamonds show strong N3 (415 nm) and H3 (503 nm) absorption.
- **CL (Cathodoluminescence) imaging:**
  - HPHT-treated diamonds show blue CL (from H3 centers) in cuboid growth sectors.
  - Natural diamonds show more varied CL colors (blue, green, yellow) depending on growth history.

### Detection Difficulty

- HPHT detection is **difficult without advanced instrumentation**. Standard gemological tools (loupe, microscope, refractometer) cannot detect HPHT treatment.
- The polariscope screening test is useful but not conclusive.
- Definitive determination requires either DiamondView imaging or low-temperature PL spectroscopy.
- Some HPHT treatments are designed to be undetectable — e.g., very low-temperature HPHT on Type Ia diamonds may produce subtle changes that are very hard to identify.
- HPHT treatment followed by irradiation can produce complex, layered color origins that require expert interpretation.

## 8. Polymer / Resin Impregnation

Polymer impregnation (also called stabilization) involves saturating a porous gemstone with a polymer resin (epoxy, polyester, or acrylic) under vacuum and/or pressure to improve durability, color, and stability. This is a major treatment for porous or friable gem materials.

### Affected Species

- **Turquoise** (most common — epoxy or polyester resin stabilization; MOST turquoise on the market is stabilized)
- **Jadeite** (B-jade — polymer impregnated to fill porous white areas; a controversial treatment that significantly alters the stone)
- **Opal** (matrix opal stabilization — resin impregnation to hold fragile matrix together; sugar-acid treated opal also sometimes receives resin)
- **Lapis Lazuli** (some stabilization with wax or polymer)
- **Malachite** (polymer impregnation to fill fractures and stabilize)
- **Serpentine** (often polymer stabilized)
- **Amazonite** (occasional stabilization)
- **Chrysoprase** (occasional stabilization)
- **Variscite** (often stabilized)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–45x)

- **UV fluorescence — KEY for B-jade:**
  - **B-jade (polymer-impregnated jadeite):** Strong whitish-yellow, greenish-white, or blue-white fluorescence under LWUV, especially concentrated in the polymer-filled areas. Natural (A-jade) is typically inert or shows very weak fluorescence.
  - **Stabilized turquoise:** Polymer-filled areas fluoresce white, yellowish-white, or blue-white under LWUV. Natural, unstabilized turquoise is usually inert.
- **Luster difference:** Stabilized areas often have a different luster than untreated areas:
  - Polymer-filled areas: Waxy, dull, resinous luster.
  - Natural jadeite surface: Vitreous luster.
  - In B-jade, the surface may appear "plastic-like" or greasy.
- **Magnification — flow patterns and bubbles:**
  - **Flow lines:** The polymer may show flow patterns (striae, swirls) visible under oblique illumination.
  - **Gas bubbles:** Trapped air bubbles in the polymer — round, spherical, varying in size.
  - **Trapped air at surface:** Air may be trapped between the polymer and the stone at surface openings.
- **Surface texture:** B-jade often shows an "orange peel" or "cobblestone" surface texture due to shrinkage of the polymer during curing.
- **Hot point test (rarely used, destructive):** A heated needle applied to a filled area melts the polymer and produces a plastic smell. **Destructive** — use only with permission.
- **Weight difference:** Heavily impregnated stones may have a noticeably lower specific gravity than natural material. For example, B-jade has SG ~3.25–3.34 vs. A-jade SG ~3.30–3.38.

### Advanced Detection

- **FTIR spectroscopy (DEFINITIVE for B-jade):**
  - **B-jade:** Strong, characteristic absorption bands from the polymer in the mid-infrared region:
    - C-H stretching: 2800–3000 cm⁻¹ (multiple sharp peaks: 2870, 2930, 2960 cm⁻¹ for epoxy/polyester)
    - C=O stretching: ~1735 cm⁻¹ (if polyester)
    - C-O-C stretching: ~1250, ~1100 cm⁻¹
    - Aromatic ring vibrations: ~1600, ~1500 cm⁻¹
  - **A-jade (natural, untreated jadeite):** Shows only jadeite silicate peaks (950–1100 cm⁻¹ Si-O stretching, 400–600 cm⁻¹ bending modes) and possible water peaks (~3400 cm⁻¹).
  - The FTIR spectrum of B-jade is so distinctive that it is considered definitive proof.
- **Raman spectroscopy:**
  - Polymer-filled areas show strong fluorescence background with Raman peaks from the polymer.
  - The jadeite Raman peaks (e.g., 377, 432, 698, 1038 cm⁻¹) may be partially obscured by polymer fluorescence.
  - In some cases, the polymer Raman spectrum can identify the specific resin formulation.
- **X-ray CT scanning:** Can map the three-dimensional distribution of polymer within the stone — useful for quantifying the extent of treatment.
- **SEM-EDS:** Backscatter electron imaging shows polymer-filled areas as dark (low atomic number) regions within the brighter jadeite matrix.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Heavily impregnated (B-jade, stabilized turquoise):** Easy to detect with UV fluorescence and FTIR.
- **Light stabilization:** Very difficult — the polymer content may be below the detection threshold of FTIR. UV fluorescence may show subtle or no difference.
- **Surface-only treatment:** Some treatments only apply polymer to the surface layer — detection requires careful FTIR or Raman analysis of the surface.
- **Most turquoise on the market today is stabilized** — disclosure is the norm, but the extent of stabilization varies enormously.

## 9. Laser Drilling

Laser drilling is a treatment specific to diamond. A microscopic laser beam creates a tiny tunnel (5–10 μm diameter) from the diamond's surface to a dark inclusion (usually a sulfide or graphite inclusion). The inclusion material is then bleached or removed with acid, and the tunnel may be left open or filled with glass.

### Affected Species

- **Diamond** (exclusively — used to remove or whiten dark inclusions to improve clarity grade)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 20x–70x)

- **Straight, narrow tunnels:** Characteristic laser drill holes appear as extremely straight, narrow channels reaching from the surface to an inclusion. They are much narrower than natural fractures or cleavages.
- **Surface openings:** The tunnel opening at the surface is typically a tiny, round or oval pit. May be visible at 20–30x as a small indentation or "pinpoint."
- **"Funnel" shape:** The tunnel may be slightly wider near the surface and taper toward the inclusion.
- **Whitened inclusion:** The target inclusion often appears whitened, bleached, or partially removed. A dark sulfide inclusion may appear white/gray after acid treatment.
- **Filled drill holes:** Some laser-drilled diamonds have the drill hole filled with high-RI glass to make the tunnel less visible. Filled drill holes may show:
  - **Flash effect** (like fracture filling) — colored interference colors in the filled tunnel.
  - **Gas bubbles** inside the filled tunnel.
  - **Partial filling** — the tunnel may be only partially filled.
- **Multiple tunnels:** Large diamonds with several dark inclusions may have multiple laser drill holes.
- **Surface residue:** White or yellowish residue may be present at the surface opening from the acid treatment.

### Advanced Detection

- **FTIR microscopy:** Can detect residual glass filler in filled drill holes.
- **Raman microscopy:** Can identify the target inclusion's alteration products (e.g., sulfide converted to sulfate).
- **X-ray micro-CT:** 3D visualization of the drill hole trajectory — definitive.

### Detection Difficulty

- Open, unfilled laser drill holes are **easy to detect** with standard gemological microscopes at 40–70x.
- Filled laser drill holes are **more difficult** — the filler matches the diamond's appearance more closely. Look for subtle flash effects and gas bubbles.
- **KT-450 technology (KM laser drilling):** A more advanced technique that uses a laser to create subsurface fractures around an inclusion without a surface-reaching tunnel. The inclusion is then bleached through the fracture network. **Very difficult to detect** — requires careful microscope examination for unusual fracture patterns.

### Disclosure Requirements

- Laser drilling must be disclosed in most jurisdictions.
- Filled laser drill holes require additional disclosure of the filling material.
- KT-450 treated diamonds are particularly controversial — some laboratories consider them "natural clarity enhanced" while others treat them as laser-drilled.

## 10. Bleaching

Bleaching uses chemical agents (hydrogen peroxide, sodium hypochlorite, oxalic acid, etc.) to lighten, whiten, or remove unwanted color from gemstones. It is most commonly applied to porous or organic gem materials.

### Affected Species

- **Pearl** (most common — bleaching to lighten yellow/cream pearls to white; nearly universal for cultured pearls)
- **Coral** (bleaching to produce white or pastel colors)
- **Ivory** (bleaching to whiten)
- **Jadeite** (bleaching of brown/dark areas — sometimes combined with polymer impregnation in B-jade processing)
- **Lapis Lazuli** (bleaching of calcite veins — occasional)
- **Turquoise** (bleaching of dark matrix — occasional)
- **Shell** (bleaching of mother-of-pearl)

### Visual Detection

- **UV fluorescence:** Bleached pearls often show a distinctive **greenish-blue** or **bluish-white** fluorescence under LWUV — distinct from natural pearl fluorescence (typically yellowish-white or blue). The fluorescence is caused by residual bleaching agents.
- **Surface etching:** Strong bleaching can produce microscopic surface etching or "chalky" areas on pearl nacre.
- **Natural color removal:** Bleached pearls lack the subtle body color variations of natural beads. They are often uniformly white with no overtone colors.
- **Concentric ring inspection:** In bleached cultured pearls, the concentric growth rings (visible through the drill hole) may show a lighter overall color than the natural surface color.
- **Crossed polarizers:** Bleaching does not affect the polariscope reaction.

### Advanced Detection

- **X-ray luminescence:** Bleached pearls may show different X-ray fluorescence patterns than unbleached pearls.
- **UV-Vis spectroscopy:** Bleached pearls show reduced or absent natural pigment absorption bands (e.g., the porphyrin bands in natural pink pearls).
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Can detect residual bleaching agents in some cases.
- **Chemical analysis (destructive):** Detection of residual chlorine or other bleaching agents in the stone — destructive, research only.

### Detection Difficulty

- Bleaching is **extremely difficult to detect definitively** in many cases.
- **Pearl bleaching is so universal** that most cultured pearls on the market are bleached to some degree — it is considered an accepted part of pearl processing, not always separately disclosed.
- No single test is definitive — identification relies on the absence of evidence for natural color and knowledge of the typical appearance of the natural material.

## 11. Lattice Diffusion (Beryllium Treatment)

Lattice diffusion (often called "beryllium diffusion" or "Be-diffusion" for sapphire) involves diffusing a small element (beryllium) into the corundum crystal lattice at very high temperatures (~1800°C) to produce colors that are stable and distributed throughout the stone — unlike surface diffusion.

### Affected Species

- **Sapphire** (most common — produces yellow, orange, padparadscha, and pink colors)
- **Ruby** (less common — beryllium diffusion to lighten dark ruby or produce orange-red)
- **Spinel** (beryllium diffusion reported but rare)

### The Beryllium Diffusion Process

- Beryllium (Be²⁺) is an extremely small ion that can diffuse rapidly into the corundum lattice at high temperature.
- The source of beryllium can be from the heating environment (e.g., chrysoberyl fragments mixed with the corundum in the crucible) or from direct application of Be compounds.
- High temperature (~1800°C) for extended periods causes Be to penetrate the entire crystal (for small stones) or to form a deep diffusion layer (for larger stones).
- The resulting colors (yellow, orange, pink) are stable and NOT limited to the surface — they are true lattice colors caused by Be interacting with other trace elements (Fe, Cr, Mg).

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 10x–45x)

- **Immersion microscopy:** Unlike surface diffusion, Be-diffused corundum shows color throughout the stone, not just at the surface. However, in larger stones, the Be penetration may be incomplete, showing a "color halo" — the stone is colored near the surface and paler in the center.
- **Abraded facet edges:** Since Be-diffused color extends deeper than surface diffusion, abraded facet edges do not typically show the dramatic "colorless core" seen in surface-diffused stones. However, in stones with incomplete penetration, worn facets may be paler.
- **Color consistency:** Be-diffused stones often have unusually uniform color — lacking the natural color zoning expected in sapphire.
- **"Glow" or fluorescence:** Some Be-diffused yellow sapphires show an unusual greenish-yellow fluorescence under LWUV — not seen in natural yellow sapphire.
- **Girdle examination:** The girdle may show darker color concentration where Be has diffused in more readily.

### Definitive Detection

- **LA-ICP-MS — THE definitive method:**
  - Beryllium is present at detectable levels (typically >1 ppm) in Be-diffused corundum.
  - Natural corundum contains <0.1 ppm Be (below detection limits of LA-ICP-MS).
  - **Depth profiling:** Ablating from surface to interior shows Be concentration that is either:
    - **Uniform** (fully penetrated small stones) — requires comparison with expected natural levels.
    - **Decreasing from surface** (partially penetrated large stones) — diagnostic of diffusion.
  - The combination of elevated Be + elevated Fe + appropriate color is diagnostic.
- **SIMS (Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry):** Can detect Be at ppb levels and create depth profiles. More sensitive than LA-ICP-MS but less widely available.
- **XRF:** Standard XRF **cannot detect Be** (atomic number 4 — below the detection window of most ED-XRF instruments). Beryllium detection requires specialized XRF with Be window capability or WDXRF.
- **ICP-QMS (solution ICP-MS):** Destructive — requires powdering a sample — but definitive for Be quantification.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Easy:** Be-diffused sapphire with unusual color, abnormally uniform color, and detectable Be by LA-ICP-MS.
- **Difficult:** Stones with natural orange/pink color that also contain trace Be from geological processes. This is rare but has been reported.
- **Very difficult:** Detecting Be-diffusion in a stone that has been subsequently heat-treated again — the diffusion profile may be modified.
- **Screening challenge:** Not all gemological laboratories have LA-ICP-MS. Screening relies on:
  - Unusual color (too "perfect" for natural)
  - Abnormal UV fluorescence
  - Suspiciously uniform color
  - Detection by a reference laboratory

## 12. Sugar-Acid Treatment (Opal)

Sugar-acid treatment is a specific treatment for opal (typically Ethiopian or Mexican opal) to darken the body color, making the play-of-color more visible against a darker background.

### Affected Species

- **Opal** (Ethiopian hydrophane opal, Mexican opal, and occasionally Australian opal)

### The Process

1. The opal is soaked in a sugar solution (or honey) for several days.
2. The stone is then heated in concentrated sulfuric acid, which carbonizes the sugar trapped in the opal's pores, leaving black carbon particles throughout the stone.
3. This produces a dark body color (black, dark gray, or dark brown) against which the play-of-color is more dramatic.

### Visual Detection

- **"Lizard skin" texture:** The treated surface often shows a distinctive "lizard skin" or "patchy" texture — irregular dark and light patches visible at 10–20x magnification. This is caused by uneven carbon deposition.
- **Carbon concentration in cracks:** Black carbon clusters along cracks, fissures, and surface pits — visible under magnification.
- **Spotty body color:** The dark body color is often uneven — darker in some areas, lighter in others — unlike natural black opal, which has a uniform body tone.
- **Weight and SG:** Sugar-acid treated opal may have a slightly lower SG than natural material due to the porous structure.
- **UV fluorescence:** The carbon filling often quenches fluorescence — treated opal shows weaker or no fluorescence compared to natural black opal.
- **Acetone test (non-destructive):** Acetone may dissolve some of the carbon/sugar residues, especially if the treatment was recent.
- **Underwater test:** Ethiopian hydrophane opal is naturally porous and water-absorbent. Sugar-acid treated opal may be less absorbent due to carbon filling of pores.
- **Chelsea filter:** Not diagnostic.

### Advanced Detection

- **Raman spectroscopy:** Detects amorphous carbon (broad D and G bands at ~1350 and ~1580 cm⁻¹) in the treated areas. Natural black opal does not contain amorphous carbon.
- **FTIR spectroscopy:** Shows absorption from organic residues (C-H stretching, C=O from oxidized sugars).
- **SEM imaging:** Shows carbon particles filling the pores and cracks.
- **Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, destructive):** Heating the stone in a controlled atmosphere reveals weight loss from carbon combustion — destructive.

### Detection Difficulty

- Sugar-acid treated opal is generally **moderately easy to detect** with careful microscope examination for the characteristic lizard-skin texture and carbon-filled cracks.
- Well-done treatments on uniform material can be harder — Raman spectroscopy is recommended for confirmation.
- Some "black opal" from Ethiopia is naturally dark (not treated). ED-XRF can show elevated Fe, K, and other elements in natural dark opal — distinguishing it from treated material.

## 13. Composite / Assembled Stones

Composite stones (also called assembled stones, doublets, triplets, or false doublets) are constructed from two or more layers of different materials bonded together. The goal is usually to simulate a much more valuable gemstone.

### Types of Composites

| Type | Construction | Common Imitations |
|---|---|---|
| Doublet | Two layers (crown + pavilion) bonded at the girdle | Opal doublet (opal top + black backing), garnet-topped doublet |
| Triplet | Three layers (crown + middle + pavilion) | Opal triplet (quartz top + opal slice + black backing), emerald triplet (quartz + green layer + quartz) |
| Cap doublet | Crown is a thin slice of genuine stone, pavilion is glass or synthetic | Corundum cap + glass pavilion |
| False doublet | Entirely synthetic or imitation materials bonded together | Various |

### Affected "Species" (Simulated)

- **Opal** (opal doublets and triplets — the most common composite; thin slice of precious opal backed by black plastic or potch, often with a quartz cap)
- **Emerald** (emerald triplet — green-dyed layer between two pieces of colorless quartz or synthetic spinel)
- **Ruby, Sapphire** (corundum cap doublet — thin ruby/sapphire crown fused to a glass or synthetic spinel pavilion)
- **Jadeite** (jadeite doublet — thin slice of good quality jade on top of poorer quality base)
- **Diamond** (diamond doublet — thin diamond crown on CZ or synthetic rutile pavilion — RARE)
- **Turquoise** (reconstituted turquoise — crushed turquoise bonded with resin)
- **Lapis Lazuli** (reconstituted lapis bonded with resin)

### Visual Detection (Microscope)

- **Girdle junction — KEY diagnostic:** The bond line between layers is visible at the girdle. Rotate the stone and examine the girdle edge — the junction appears as a straight line or slight discontinuity.
- **Bubbles in adhesive:** Air bubbles trapped in the bonding agent (usually colorless or slightly yellow epoxy) along the join line.
- **Luster/color discontinuity:** The two layers may have different luster or polish quality. Under reflected light, the crown and pavilion may appear different.
- ****Different inclusion populations:**** The crown layer may have natural inclusions while the pavilion has synthetic or glass bubbles — a dead giveaway.
- **UV fluorescence difference:** The two layers may fluoresce differently. For example, a natural diamond crown (blue fluorescence) on a CZ pavilion (inert).
- **Refractometer reading — KEY detection:**
  - **Opal doublet/triplet:** Read through the crown (quartz cap) shows RI ~1.544–1.553 (quartz). Read on the side/pavilion may show different RI.
  - **Emerald triplet:** Crown and pavilion give quartz RI (~1.544–1.553) or synthetic spinel RI (~1.728).
  - **Corundum cap doublet:** Crown gives corundum RI (~1.760–1.768), but pavilion gives glass RI (~1.50–1.70) or synthetic spinel RI.
- **Spectroscope:** The emission/absorption spectrum of the crown and pavilion may differ. An emerald triplet may show a natural emerald spectrum through the crown but nothing through the pavilion.
- **Immersion test:** Immerse in methylene iodide or benzyl benzoate — the different layers become visible as color/relief differences, and the bond line is accentuated.
- **Hot point test (destructive):** The adhesive may soften or melt when heated — rarely used.

### Advanced Detection

- **X-ray radiography:** Different layers have different X-ray opacity, making the junction visible.
- **Micro-CT:** 3D imaging of the internal structure clearly reveals the layers.
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Each layer can be analyzed independently — the opal layer will show an opal spectrum (amorphous silica, broad band at ~450 cm⁻¹) while the quartz cap will show a crystalline quartz spectrum (sharp peaks at 465, 206, 128 cm⁻¹).
- **LA-ICP-MS:** Trace element profiles of each layer confirm the different origins.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Easy:** Well-made doublets/triplets are still detectable by careful girdle examination and RI measurement.
- **Moderate:** Very thin bond lines with well-matched materials may require immersion microscopy.
- **Difficult:** Some modern synthetic composites can be very convincing. Raman spectroscopy of each layer is definitive.

## 14. Waxing / Surface Coating (Jadeite, Turquoise)

Waxing involves impregnating or coating a gemstone with wax (natural beeswax, paraffin, or synthetic waxes) to improve luster, fill surface pores, or deepen color. This is different from polymer impregnation (which uses hard, durable resins) — waxing is less permanent.

### Affected Species

- **Jadeite** (traditional waxing — surface coating to enhance luster; often called "A-jade with wax" — accepted when light)
- **Turquoise** (waxing to improve color and luster — common)
- **Lapis Lazuli** (waxing to darken blue color and fill surface pores — very common)
- **Serpentine** (waxing to improve appearance)
- **Variscite** (waxing)

### Visual Detection

- **UV fluorescence:** Wax fluoresces white, yellowish-white, or blue-white under LWUV. Heavily waxed areas fluoresce strongly.
- **Surface feel:** Waxed stones may feel slightly greasy or waxy to the touch — distinct from the "cold" feel of an unwaxed stone.
- **Luster:** Wax imparts a slightly softer, more resinous luster compared to the vitreous luster of a clean-polished surface.
- **Magnification:** Under reflected light at 20–40x, wax may be visible as a thin, slightly uneven coating on the surface with a soft, flowing texture.
- **Scratch test:** A fingernail or soft metal point may leave a mark on a heavily waxed surface (the wax scratches, not the stone).
- **Heat test:** Holding a waxed stone near a heat source (not flame!) may cause the wax to "sweat" or develop surface condensation — but this is subtle.
- **Acetone wipe test:** A cotton swab with acetone may dissolve surface wax. The swab may feel greasy after. Heavily waxed stones may show color pickup if the wax contains dye.

### Advanced Detection

- **FTIR spectroscopy:** Shows C-H stretching peaks (2850, 2920 cm⁻¹) from the wax's hydrocarbon chains. Natural (unwaxed) jadeite shows only silicate peaks.
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Wax produces characteristic C-H stretching and bending peaks, plus a strong fluorescence background.

### Detection Difficulty

- **Easy:** Heavily waxed stones show strong UV fluorescence and visible surface coating.
- **Difficult:** Light waxing (a common and accepted practice for jadeite) may be difficult to detect without surface-sensitive FTIR.
- Many "A-jade" pendants and bangles on the market are lightly waxed — this is generally considered a finishing/polishing step rather than a treatment.

## 15. Cavity Filling

Cavity filling involves filling surface-reaching cavities, pits, or holes in a gemstone with a foreign material (glass, resin, wax, or oil) to improve apparent clarity. This overlaps with fracture filling but targets isolated cavities rather than continuous fractures.

### Affected Species

- **Diamond** (cavities filled with glass or resin — less common since fracture filling became dominant)
- **Ruby** (surface pits filled with glass — common alongside glass filling)
- **Sapphire** (similar cavity filling)
- **Emerald** (cavities filled with oil or resin)
- **Tourmaline** (surface cavities filled with resin)
- **Topaz** (surface cavities filled)
- **Garnet** (surface cavities filled)
- **Quartz** (vug and cavity filling)

### Visual Detection (Microscope, 20x–70x)

- **Surface pits with filler:** Under reflected light, filled cavities appear as smooth areas (filler) within rougher natural pits. The filler often has a different luster than the surrounding stone.
- **Flow lines and bubbles:** The filler material may show flow patterns (if it was molten) or trapped gas bubbles (if glass or resin).
- **Color difference:** The filler may be a different color than the host stone:
  - Colorless filler in a colored stone — looks like a "window" into the cavity.
  - Colored filler — used to make the cavity less noticeable.
- **Meniscus at cavity opening:** The filler surface may be concave (suggesting liquid origin) or convex (suggesting overfilling).
- **Partial fill:** Some cavities may be only partially filled, with an empty void visible below the filler surface.
- **UV fluorescence:** Filler may fluoresce differently than the host stone.
- **"Bridging":** In some filled cavities, the filler bridges across the cavity opening, creating a thin film that can be broken easily.

### Advanced Detection

- **XRF:** Identifies filler composition (lead glass, resin, etc.).
- **Raman spectroscopy:** Identifies filler material.
- **Micro-CT:** 3D mapping of the cavity and filler distribution.

## Instrument-Specific Detection Protocols

### Microscope (70x, darkfield + fiber-optic)

**Screening checklist — examine systematically:**

1. **Darkfield illumination** (40x): Note inclusion types, sizes, distribution, phases (solid, liquid, gas).
2. **Fiber-optic overhead** (20x): Scan for flash effects in fractures (rotate stone slowly).
3. **Oblique fiber-optic** (40x): Look for surface coating wear, luster differences.
4. **UV epi-illumination** (if available): UV fluorescence patterns in fractures.
5. **Immersion cell** (for suspect diffusion): Submerge in appropriate liquid; examine color distribution.

**Key questions to answer:**
- Are inclusions pristine (natural/untreated) or altered (heat)?
- Are fractures empty or filled? Do they flash?
- Is color uniform or concentrated in fractures (dye)?
- Is there a surface coating visible at facet edges?
- Are there surface-reaching tunnels (laser drilling)?

### UV Lamp (LW 365 nm / SW 254 nm)

**Screening protocol:**

1. **LWUV (365 nm):** Observe fluorescence color, intensity, and distribution.
   - Fracture fluorescence (yellow-green) → oiling/resin in emerald.
   - White fracture fluorescence → polymer impregnation (B-jade, stabilized turquoise).
   - Unnatural strong color → possible dye.
   - Geometric growth patterns → HPHT diamond (requires DiamondView for resolution).

2. **SWUV (254 nm):** Check for differences — many treatments fluoresce differently under SW vs. LW.
   - Diamond: Blue fluorescence common, but HPHT-treated may show green-yellow.
   - Some polymer fillers fluoresce under LW but not SW.

3. **Confidence score:** 1 (no fluorescence) to 5 (diagnostic fluorescence pattern).

### FTIR Spectroscopy

**Applications by region:**

| Region (cm⁻¹) | Diagnostic For |
|---|---|
| 3000–3700 | OH stretching in corundum (heat treatment); water in beryl |
| 2800–3000 | C-H stretching in oils, resins, polymers, waxes (oiling, B-jade, stabilization) |
| 1700–1800 | C=O stretching in polyesters (resin fillers) |
| 1500–1700 | Aromatic ring vibrations (Opticon, some resins) |
| 1000–1500 | Fingerprint region — filler identification |
| 400–1100 | Silicate matrix absorption (host mineral) |

**Protocol:**
1. Run background scan.
2. Position stone on ATR crystal (or use reflection mode for mounted stones).
3. Collect spectrum (400–4000 cm⁻¹).
4. Subtract host mineral reference spectrum.
5. Identify residual peaks — match to known filler database.

### Raman Spectroscopy

**Applications:**

- **Inclusion identification:** Identify mineral inclusions and check for thermal alteration phases.
- **Filler identification:** Match unknown filler spectra to reference database (glass, resin, oil, wax, dye).
- **Coating analysis:** Raman can penetrate thin coatings to identify the coating material.
- **Phase identification:** Differentiate polymorphs (e.g., rutile vs. anatase TiO₂ in coatings).
- **Diamond type:** Single-crystal diamond shows characteristic first-order Raman peak at 1332 cm⁻¹; shifts and broadening indicate strain (HPHT treatment).

**Protocol:**
1. Use 514 nm or 785 nm laser (785 nm reduces fluorescence background for organic materials).
2. Focus on suspicious area (fracture, inclusion, coating).
3. Collect spectrum (100–2000 cm⁻¹ range).
4. Compare with reference database.
5. For fluorescence-dominated spectra (common for organic fillers), try 1064 nm FT-Raman.

### ED-XRF

**Applications:**

- **Elemental detection for filler identification:** Pb, Bi, Ba, Sr in glass fillers.
- **Surface diffusion screening:** Elevated Fe, Ti, Cr at surface vs. interior.
- **Coating analysis:** Ti, Al, Si, Zr from oxide coatings.
- **Gem species identification:** Trace element fingerprint.
- **Geographic origin:** Major + trace element profiles.

**Limitations:**
- Cannot detect **Be** (atomic number 4) — use LA-ICP-MS for beryllium diffusion.
- Cannot detect **organic elements** (C, H, O, N) — use Raman/FTIR for fillers.
- Surface-sensitive — may not represent bulk composition.

### LA-ICP-MS

**Applications:**

- **Beryllium diffusion detection:** Be quantification in corundum.
- **Depth profiling:** Diffusion profile characterization.
- **Geographic origin determination:** Trace element fingerprint.
- **Heat treatment assessment:** Element distribution changes.

**Limitations:**
- **Destructive** (leaves a 20–50 μm crater).
- Requires calibration standards.
- Not widely available in commercial gem labs.

### DiamondView

**Applications:**

- **HPHT detection:** Geometric growth patterns vs. natural irregular patterns.
- **HPHT synthetic identification:** Distinct sector zoning patterns.
- **Natural vs. synthetic diamond:** Growth pattern differences.
- **Treatment history:** Fluorescence patterns reveal growth and treatment history.

**Limitations:**
- Diamond-specific instrument (only useful for diamond).
- Very expensive — only in major gemological laboratories.
- Requires interpretation by trained personnel.

## Practical Detection Workflow

This workflow is designed for a gemologist presented with an unknown faceted stone. It assumes access to a standard gemological lab (microscope, UV lamp, refractometer, polariscope, spectroscope) and optionally FTIR/Raman.

### Level 1: Basic Screening (5–10 minutes)

**Goal:** Identify obvious treatments and narrow possibilities.

1. **Identify the species first:**
   - Measure RI, birefringence, optic character (refractometer, polariscope).
   - Measure specific gravity (hydrostatic balance or heavy liquids).
   - Observe spectrum (handheld spectroscope or UV-Vis).
   - Observe inclusions (microscope, darkfield).

2. **UV fluorescence check:**
   - LWUV (365 nm): Note color and distribution.
   - SWUV (254 nm): Note differences from LW.
   - **Key flags:** Fracture fluorescence (oil/resin), uniform bright fluorescence (polymer impreg.), patchy fluorescence (dye).

3. **Microscope scan:**
   - **Darkfield:** General inclusion survey.
   - **Fiber-optic (overhead, rotating):** Flash effect in fractures (glass filling).
   - **High magnification (40–70x):** Surface details — coating wear, dye concentration, drill holes, filled cavities.

4. **Polariscope (for diamond):**
   - Strong strain (natural) vs. weak/absent strain (HPHT-treated) — screening only.

### Level 2: Intermediate Testing (10–30 minutes)

**Goal:** Confirm or rule out specific treatments based on Level 1 results.

5. **If fracture fluorescence detected (emerald, jadeite, turquoise):**
   - FTIR or Raman for filler identification.
   - Acetone wipe test (if surface dye suspected).

6. **If flash effect detected (ruby, diamond):**
   - ED-XRF spot analysis at fracture (Pb, Bi detection).
   - Raman spectroscopy of filler material.

7. **If suspect surface diffusion (sapphire, ruby):**
   - Immersion microscopy: Observe color distribution.
   - Examine facet edges for wear revealing lighter interior.
   - ED-XRF surface vs. interior comparison.

8. **If suspect heat treatment (corundum):**
   - Examine silk dissolution, discoid halos.
   - FTIR OH region — check 3309 cm⁻¹ peak intensity.
   - Raman of mineral inclusions for phase alterations.

9. **If suspect laser drilling (diamond):**
   - High-magnification scan of surface for drill hole openings.
   - Track tunnels from surface to inclusions.
   - Check for glass filling in tunnels (flash effect).

10. **If suspect composite:**
    - RI reading on crown and pavilion (should be identical for natural stone).
    - Girdle scan at 40x for bond line.
    - Immersion test.

### Level 3: Advanced Lab Confirmation

**Goal:** Definitive identification for ambiguous or high-value cases.

11. **FTIR spectroscopy:**
    - For resin/oil/wax/polymer identification in fractures.
    - For OH region in corundum (heat treatment).
    - For nitrogen aggregation in diamond (HPHT).

12. **Raman spectroscopy:**
    - For filler/coating/dye identification.
    - For inclusion phase identification.
    - For opal treatment confirmation (amorphous carbon).

13. **ED-XRF:**
    - For heavy elements in fillers (Pb, Bi, Sr).
    - For surface element concentration (diffusion).

14. **PL spectroscopy (diamond):**
    - Low-temperature (LN₂) PL for color center identification.
    - GR1 (741 nm) for irradiation; H3/H4 for HPHT; NV for irradiation.

15. **LA-ICP-MS:**
    - Beryllium detection for Be-diffused sapphire.
    - Depth profiling for diffusion confirmation.
    - Trace element fingerprint for origin.

### Decision Tree Summary

```
Unknown stone
  → Identify species (RI, SG, spectrum)
  → UV fluorescence → suspicious? → FTIR/Raman
  → Microscope flash effect? → XRF/Raman
  → Immersion test (color distribution) → surface vs. body color
  → For diamond: polariscope → DiamondView → PL
  → For sapphire/ruby: FTIR (3309 cm⁻¹) → LA-ICP-MS (if Be suspected)
  → For emerald: UV fluorescence → FTIR for resin type
  → For pearl: UV fluorescence → X-ray (cultured vs. natural)
  → For jadeite: UV fluorescence → FTIR (B-jade definitive)
```

## FTC Guidelines and Disclosure Summary

The FTC Jewelry Guides (16 CFR Part 23) establish requirements for the disclosure of gemstone treatments. Key provisions:

### General Requirements

- **§ 23.23(a):** A seller must disclose if a treatment **"has a significant effect on the stone's value"** — and if the treatment is not permanent or creates special care requirements.
- **§ 23.23(b):** Disclosure must be made **before the sale** — on invoices, appraisals, and advertisements.

| Treatment | FTC Disclosure Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Not explicitly required for corundum (accepted) | Recommended for disclosure; CIBJO requires disclosure |
| Glass filling | **Yes** — must disclose | "Glass-filled ruby" |
| Oiling/Resin | **Yes** for heavy oiling; moderate for light | "Emerald — clarity enhanced with resin" |
| Irradiation | **Yes** — must disclose | "Blue topaz — irradiated" |
| Dyeing | **Yes** — must disclose | "Jadeite — color enhanced by dyeing" |
| Surface diffusion | **Yes** — must disclose | "Sapphire — surface diffused color" |
| Coating | **Yes** — must disclose | "Coated tanzanite" |

- **Glass-filled ruby:** Must disclose — considered a composite material by some laboratories.

## Glossary of Key Terms

- **ATR-FTIR:** Attenuated Total Reflectance Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy — surface-sensitive FTIR technique ideal for coatings and surface treatments.
- **B-jade:** Jadeite that has been polymer impregnated (usually bleached + polymer filled). "B" stands for "bleached" or "bonded."
- **Color center:** A crystal lattice defect (vacancy, interstitial, or impurity) that absorbs visible light, giving color to the stone.
- **DiamondView:** UV fluorescence imaging system from the Diamond Trading Company (De Beers) that reveals growth and treatment patterns in diamond.
- **Discoid halo:** A circular or oval fracture halo around a mineral inclusion, caused by differential thermal expansion during heating.
- **EPR (ESR):** Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (also called Electron Spin Resonance) — detects unpaired electrons in color centers. Definitive for irradiation detection.
- **Flash effect:** Iridescent color flashes (blue, orange, green) seen in glass-filled fractures under fiber-optic illumination — pathognomonic for glass filling.
- **GR1:** General Radiation 1 — a zero-phonon absorption line at 741 nm in diamond, caused by a neutral vacancy. Definitive for irradiation treatment.
- **H3 center:** A nitrogen-vacancy-nitrogen defect in diamond producing zero-phonon lines at 503 nm (absorption) and 503 nm (PL). Enhanced by HPHT treatment.
- **HPHT:** High Pressure High Temperature — treatment using extreme pressure and heat (50–70 kbar, 1300–2000°C).
- **Hydrophane opal:** A porous variety of opal (typically from Ethiopia) that absorbs water and becomes transparent/translucent when wet. Susceptible to sugar-acid treatment.
- **Immersion microscopy:** Viewing a gemstone submerged in a liquid with similar refractive index to eliminate surface reflections and reveal internal features.
- **LA-ICP-MS:** Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry — a destructive trace element analysis technique that can depth-profile element concentrations.
- **Lattice diffusion:** Penetration of small elements (Be, Mg) into the crystal lattice at high temperature, creating body color (not just surface color). Distinguished from surface diffusion.
- **Metamict:** A state of partial amorphization (loss of crystal structure) caused by radiation damage, common in zircon. Heating can recrystallize metamict material.
- **NV center:** Nitrogen-Vacancy center in diamond — a nitrogen atom adjacent to a lattice vacancy. Produces zero-phonon lines at 575 nm (NV⁰) and 637 nm (NV⁻). Created or enhanced by irradiation + annealing.
- **Pathognomonic:** A sign or symptom that is so characteristic that it is diagnostic of a particular condition — e.g., the flash effect is pathognomonic for glass-filled ruby.
- **PL spectroscopy:** Photoluminescence spectroscopy — measuring light emitted by a material after excitation with a laser. Used extensively for diamond color center analysis.
- **Silk:** Microscopic needle-like rutile (TiO₂) inclusions in corundum. Silk dissolution is a key indicator of heat treatment.
- **Type IIa diamond:** Diamond with no measurable nitrogen impurities (the purest type, <5 ppm N). The most common type for HPHT color improvement.
- **Umbrella effect:** Darker blue color at the pavilion tip (culet) of irradiated topaz, fading toward the crown — caused by the geometry of irradiation exposure.
- **Zoning:** Variation in color or trace element concentration within a crystal. Natural zoning is typically sharp and angular; heat-treated zoning is diffuse.
