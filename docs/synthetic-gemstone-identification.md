# Synthetic Gemstone Identification Guide

## For Distinguishing Natural vs. Lab-Grown Gems

The ability to reliably distinguish natural gemstones from their synthetic counterparts is one of the most critical skills in modern gemology. As laboratory-grown gems have become increasingly sophisticated — some requiring advanced spectroscopic or trace-element analysis to separate — the gemologist's toolkit has expanded well beyond the loupe and refractometer. This guide provides a systematic reference for the identification of synthetic gemstones across all major growth methods, organized by manufacturing technique and species.

The market context is essential: synthetic gems are not imitations or simulants. They possess the same chemical composition, crystal structure, and physical properties as their natural counterparts. A synthetic ruby is truly corundum (Al₂O₃); a synthetic emerald is truly beryl (Be₃Al₂SiO₆). The challenge lies in identifying the *fingerprints of manufacture* — the inclusions, growth features, trace-element patterns, and spectroscopic signatures imparted by each growth method. With the proliferation of high-quality flux-grown, hydrothermal, and HPHT material, the era of easy identification via curved striae alone is long past. This guide equips the reader with detection criteria ranging from simple visual checks to definitive laboratory analysis.

---

## 1. Growth Method Overview

Synthetic gemstones fall into three broad categories based on the growth technique:

**Melt Growth** — The raw material is melted and then solidified under controlled conditions. This category includes Flame Fusion (Verneuil), Czochralski (pulled growth), and Skull Melting. These methods are generally fast and economical but often leave characteristic telltales such as curved striae, gas bubbles, and metallic inclusions from crucibles.

**Solution Growth** — The raw material is dissolved in a solvent (either a molten flux or a hydrothermal aqueous solution) and then precipitated onto a seed crystal under controlled cooling or temperature gradient. This category includes Flux Growth and Hydrothermal Growth. These methods produce higher-quality crystals that more closely resemble natural material, making them significantly harder to identify. They typically lack curved striae but may exhibit flux residues, seed plates, chevron graining, or distinctive spectroscopic signatures.

**High-Pressure Growth** — Diamond synthesis requires extreme conditions to stabilize the carbon crystal structure. This category includes HPHT (High-Pressure High-Temperature) and CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition). HPHT mimics the natural diamond formation environment using a metal catalyst at high pressure and temperature. CVD grows diamond from a hydrocarbon plasma at low pressure. Both methods leave distinctive growth structures, strain patterns, and spectroscopic fingerprints.

## 2. Flame Fusion (Verneuil Method)

### Process Description

The Verneuil method, invented by Auguste Verneuil in 1902 and commercialized in the 1910s, is the oldest and most economical method for producing synthetic gemstones. Finely powdered raw material (typically alumina for corundum) is fed through a vibrating sieve into an oxyhydrogen flame at approximately 2000°C. The molten droplets fall onto a rotating pedestal, gradually building up a single-crystal boule.

Key characteristics of the process:
- **Growth rate**: 5–20 mm per hour (extremely fast by crystal-growing standards)
- **Boule shape**: Carrot-shaped or rounded club-shape, often with a narrow neck at the top where growth began and a broader rounded base
- The boule is typically 1–3 cm in diameter and 5–10 cm long
- No crucible is used — the molten material is supported by the flame itself, which eliminates crucible contamination but introduces characteristic gas bubbles
- Material purity can be controlled by the purity of the feed powder, allowing precise color control via added dopants

First used commercially in the 1900s for ruby, the method rapidly expanded to sapphire, spinel, rutile, and strontium titanate. It remains the most widely used method for low-cost synthetic gems today.

### General Identification Features

**Curved striae (growth lines)** — This is the single most diagnostic feature for flame-fusion synthetic gems, particularly in corundum. When viewed under darkfield illumination or immersion, the boule exhibits fine parallel curved bands perpendicular to the growth axis. These bands represent slight variations in refractive index caused by fluctuations in the growth temperature and feed rate during the rapid crystallization process. The striae appear as alternating light and dark curved lines that run *across* the length of the boule. In a faceted stone, they often appear in multiple directions depending on the orientation of cut — this is a key check. In natural corundum, growth lines (if present at all) are straight angular bands parallel to crystal faces, not curved.

**Gas bubbles** — Round, oval, or tadpole-shaped cavities containing gas trapped during the rapid growth. These can appear in the seed area or be distributed throughout the crystal. They vary in size from barely visible under 10× magnification (sub-10 microns) to 100 microns or more. Bubbles are often clustered, and larger bubbles may have elongated or teardrop shapes. In natural gemstones, gas bubbles are essentially nonexistent (except in some volcanic glasses). The presence of any gas bubble in a crystalline gemstone is strongly suggestive of synthetic origin.

**Unusual color for the natural analog** — Flame-fusion colors tend to be too pure, too saturated, or simply wrong for natural counterparts. For example, flame-fusion synthetic ruby often has a bright "stoplight" red with a slightly orange tint rarely seen in fine natural ruby. Flame-fusion spinel comes in intense cobalt blue, brilliant turquoise, and vivid emerald-green — colors unknown in nature for spinel. The evenness of color distribution (no color zoning) is itself suspicious for stones like sapphire that are naturally zoned.

**Clarity too clean** — Natural gemstones nearly always contain some internal features: inclusions, fractures, mineral crystals, growth features. A large, flawless, intensely colored stone at a low price is the classic red flag. Flame-fusion stones can be completely clean aside from the diagnostic features listed above.

**Strain under crossed polarizers** — The rapid growth and thermal gradient during flame fusion can produce anomalous double refraction (ADR) in isotropic materials like synthetic spinel. This appears as a tabby extinction pattern (irregular sweeps of light and dark) rather than the clean extinction of a true isotropic material.

**Chelsea Colour Filter (CCF) reaction** — Many flame-fusion corundums and spinels exhibit an exceptionally strong red fluorescence under the Chelsea filter, often brighter and more intense than natural equivalents. This can be a useful screening tool but is not definitive on its own.

### Ruby (Flame Fusion)

- **Curved striae**: Pathognomonic — always present in typical flame-fusion ruby. Visible under darkfield or immersion at 10–30× magnification. The striae run in curved parallel arcs and are the single most reliable feature.
- **Gas bubbles**: Common. Look in the seed area and distributed through the crystal. Often teardrop-shaped.
- **Color**: Bright red with a noticeable orange component, often described as "stoplight red." Natural fine ruby is typically more of a pure or slightly purplish red. Some flame-fusion ruby is doped with chromium alone, producing a very intense red.
- **UV fluorescence**: Very strong red under both long-wave and short-wave UV, often brighter and more intense than natural ruby (which can vary from inert to strong depending on origin). The fluorescence under SW is particularly strong — a useful screening test.
- **Chelsea Colour Filter**: Very strong bright red, typically more intense than natural.
- **Artificial coloring**: Occasionally, early flame-fusion ruby contained glass or other filler, but modern material is generally clean.
- **Trade names**: Linde synthetic ruby (Verneuil), Chatham synthetic ruby (which is actually flux-grown — beware of naming confusion). Most commercial flame-fusion ruby is simply sold as "synthetic ruby."
- **Market warning**: Large quantities of flame-fusion ruby are produced annually and sold in low-end jewelry. The material is so inexpensive that even high-quality parcels rarely command more than a few dollars per carat.

### Sapphire (Flame Fusion)

- **Curved striae**: Present and visible, though often less obvious than in ruby. Best viewed in immersion (using methylene iodide or monobromonaphthalene) with strong darkfield illumination. The striae appear as fine color banding.
- **Gas bubbles**: Common, sometimes in high concentration. Watch for clustered bubbles near the seed.
- **Color**: Very even, pure blue (from cobalt or titanium doping). Also produced in colorless, yellow (doped with nickel or chromium), orange, green (combined dopants), pink (chromium), and padsugaradhska (blue-green). The uniformity of color is itself suspicious — natural sapphire almost always shows some color zoning or color banding.
- **Color zoning**: Absent. This is a critical distinction from natural sapphire, where angular color zoning is the norm. If a sapphire shows no color zoning at all, suspect synthetic.
- **UV fluorescence**: LW UV often produces a chalky bluish-white or greenish fluorescence. SW UV is variable — sometimes inert, sometimes weak. The chalky LW reaction is quite characteristic.
- **Absorption spectrum**: May lack the natural iron-related absorption lines found in most blue sapphires. Natural blue sapphire typically shows iron lines at 450, 460, and 470 nm. Flame-fusion blue sapphire may show only a general absorption in the yellow-green region (from the cobalt absorption band at 550–600 nm).
- **Note on flame-fusion fancy colors**: Pink, yellow, orange, green, and purple sapphires are all produced by flame fusion. The colors are typically very even and clean. These can be harder to identify than blue, as curved striae may be less visible and natural fancy sapphires can also be clean. Check for gas bubbles and color uniformity.

### Spinel (Flame Fusion)

- **Curved striae**: Present but often less obvious than in corundum. They are finer and may require immersion and careful focusing to detect.
- **Gas bubbles**: Often abundant, and many are flattened or disc-shaped (a characteristic of flame-fusion spinel). The bubbles may have a distinctive "snowball" appearance under darkfield.
- **Color**: Flame-fusion spinel is produced in an extraordinary range of colors, including some that are completely unknown in natural spinel. Particularly diagnostic:
  - Intense cobalt blue (natural spinel is usually grayish-blue at best)
  - Bright turquoise (unmatched in nature)
  - Vivid emerald-green (natural spinel is never this green)
  - Intense yellow-orange (natural spinel is rare in these colors)
  - Bright pink and red (can resemble natural, but the color is more even and intense)
- **RI**: 1.728 — notably consistent across flame-fusion spinel. Natural spinel has a variable RI (1.712–1.736) depending on composition. An RI of exactly 1.728 ± 0.002 on a spinel that looks synthetic supports the identification.
- **SG**: 3.64 (consistent, matching stoichiometric MgAl₂O₄). Natural spinel typically ranges 3.57–3.72.
- **Anomalous double refraction (ADR)**: Very characteristic for flame-fusion synthetic spinel. Under crossed polarizers, the stone shows a patchy or "tabby" extinction pattern — irregular regions that go dark and light as the stage is rotated. This is due to strain from the rapid growth. Natural spinel, being perfectly isotropic, shows complete extinction (dark) at all angles under crossed polars. The ADR is so characteristic that a spinel with ADR can be identified as flame-fusion synthetic with high confidence.
- **Used as a simulant**: Flame-fusion spinel is frequently used to simulate other gems, including sapphire (blue spinel), aquamarine (pale blue), peridot (yellow-green), zircon (colorless), and ruby (red spinel). Always identify the species first, then determine natural vs. synthetic origin.
- **Natural vs. synthetic spinel checklist**: (1) ADR present? If yes, highly likely synthetic. (2) Color unknown in nature? If yes, synthetic. (3) Curved striae? Synthetic. (4) RI exactly 1.728? Suspicious. (5) Bubbles? Synthetic.

### Synthetic Rutile (Flame Fusion)

- **Untreated appearance**: Dark yellow-brown to dark red-brown as-grown. The as-grown boules are nearly black due to oxygen deficiency and require annealing in an oxygen-rich atmosphere to produce lighter colors.
- **Treated colors**: After annealing, synthetic rutile can be colorless, pale yellow, light blue, green, orange, or red. The colors are often distinctive and not found in natural rutile (which is typically dark red-brown to black).
- **RI**: 2.616–2.903 — the highest refractive index of any known gem material. This extreme RI produces a correspondingly high luster and brilliance.
- **Dispersion**: 0.330 — this is the highest dispersion of any gem material, more than ten times that of diamond (0.044). Synthetic rutile exhibits massive fire — it appears to sparkle with rainbow colors in any light. This extreme fire is the most immediate visual clue.
- **Birefringence**: 0.287 — extremely strong double refraction. The facet edges will appear doubled (doubling of back facets) even with the unaided eye in many cases. This is the second most obvious clue after the fire.
- **Hardness**: 6–6.5 — soft enough that facet edges wear easily. This is a practical identifier if the stone shows worn facet junctions.
- **Inclusions**: Gas bubbles and curved striae, consistent with flame fusion origin.
- **Trade names**: Titania, Kaiser's synthetic rutile. No longer widely produced for jewelry due to softness and the rise of CZ.
- **Historical note**: Synthetic rutile was introduced as a diamond simulant in the 1940s–50s but was quickly replaced by strontium titanate and later CZ due to its softness and yellow tint.

### Strontium Titanate (Flame Fusion)

- **Color**: Colorless in its pure form. Can be doped to produce pale colors, but is almost always seen colorless as a diamond simulant.
- **RI**: 2.409 — very close to diamond's 2.417. This gives strontium titanate a luster and brilliance nearly indistinguishable from diamond to the unaided eye.
- **Dispersion**: 0.190 — approximately four times diamond's dispersion. The fire is very strong and immediately noticeable. A strontium titanate will flash strong spectral colors in any moderately bright light.
- **SG**: 5.13 — significantly heavier than diamond (3.52). A strontium titanate stone feels distinctly heavier in the hand. This is a key screening test if the stone is loose.
- **Hardness**: 5–6 — very soft for a gemstone. Facet edges will show wear, and the surface may be scratched easily. Diamond has no wear.
- **Magnification**: Gas bubbles and curved striae are characteristic, though the striae may be fine. Watch for "orange peel" surface texture from the polishing process (the stone's softness makes it hard to polish perfectly).
- **Heat conductivity**: Very low — strontium titanate does not conduct heat well. A thermal probe (diamond tester) will register as "not diamond." This is the most definitive simple test.
- **UV fluorescence**: Usually inert. May show a weak whitish fluorescence under SW UV.
- **Trade names**: Fabulite, Wellington diamond, Diagem, Starilian. Also sold as "strontium titanate" or "ST" in gemological contexts.
- **Historical peak**: Widely used as a diamond simulant in the 1950s–70s. Still encountered in vintage jewelry.
- **Detection summary**: Heavy weight + extreme fire + no heat conduction + very soft + gas bubbles = strontium titanate.

## 3. Czochralski (Pulled Growth)

### Process Description

Developed by Jan Czochralski in 1916 and refined for gem production in the 1960s, the Czochralski method produces gem-quality crystals by pulling a seed crystal from a melt contained in a crucible. The raw material (typically Al₂O₃ for corundum, or Y₂O₃/Al₂O₃ for YAG) is melted in an iridium or platinum crucible at high temperature (2050°C for corundum). A seed crystal is lowered into the melt and then slowly withdrawn while rotating. The crystal grows as a cylindrical boule, typically 2–5 cm in diameter and 10–30 cm long.

Key process characteristics:
- Growth rate: 2–15 mm per hour (slower than Verneuil, faster than flux)
- Crucible material: Typically iridium (Ir) or platinum (Pt), which leaves diagnostic inclusions
- Atmosphere: Controlled — often a reducing or inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation of the crucible
- Doping: Very precise — dopants are added to the melt in exact proportions, producing highly consistent colors
- Boule shape: Perfect cylinder with rounded bottom, often with visible growth rings on the surface

The Czochralski method produces higher-quality crystals than flame fusion because the growth is slower and more controlled. The resulting material has fewer internal defects and is typically completely free of curved striae. This makes Czochralski-grown stones significantly harder to identify than flame-fusion material.

### General Identification

- **No curved striae**: This is the most important negative feature. Czochralski growth proceeds at a steady, controlled rate in a stable thermal environment, so the temperature fluctuations that cause curved striae in Verneuil material do not occur. If you suspect a stone is synthetic but cannot find curved striae, consider Czochralski or flux growth.
- **Tiny metallic inclusions from crucible**: This is the single most reliable positive identification feature. Iridium or platinum particles (typically 1–20 microns) from the crucible may become incorporated into the growing crystal. These appear as tiny, sharply defined, opaque metallic specks under high magnification (40–80×). They are often square, rectangular, or irregular in shape. They are strongly reflective and can look like tiny mirrors. These inclusions are essentially diagnostic — natural corundum does not contain iridium or platinum.
- **Occasional round bubbles**: Much rarer than in flame-fusion material, but isolated bubbles or bubble clusters can occur if gas is entrained in the melt. The bubbles are typically spherical and clear, and they are fewer in number than in Verneuil material.
- **Very clean overall**: Czochralski crystals are typically free of natural-looking inclusions such as mineral crystals, fingerprints, or silk (rutile needles). The stones are exceptionally clean.
- **Boule shape clues**: If the rough is visible, the cylindrical shape with a flat or slightly rounded bottom is characteristic. The surface of the boule may show faint longitudinal striae (pull marks) rather than the transverse curved lines of Verneuil.

### Corundum (Czochralski)

- **No curved striae**: Essential negative feature. If you have a synthetic corundum and cannot find curved striae, Czochralski is the likely growth method.
- **Metallic inclusions**: Look for tiny platinum or iridium platelets under high magnification. These are highly reflective and appear as bright metallic dots or rectangles. They are the single most important positive identifier.
- **Very clean, precise color**: Czochralski corundum has exceptionally even color distribution — more consistent than Verneuil and far more consistent than natural. The color is determined by precise doping: chromium for red (ruby), titanium + iron for blue (sapphire), nickel for yellow, cobalt for green, etc.
- **UV fluorescence**: Similar to Verneuil corundum — very strong red for ruby, chalky bluish-white for blue sapphire.
- **Absorption spectrum**: Similar to natural corundum of the same color, as the color-causing dopants are the same elements. Trace element analysis (LA-ICP-MS) would be needed to distinguish from natural, looking at ratios of Ga/Mg/Fe/Ti/V.
- **Detection difficulty**: Hard to very hard. Without high magnification to spot metallic inclusions, Czochralski corundum can be nearly impossible to distinguish from natural corundum without advanced laboratory testing.

### YAG (Yttrium Aluminum Garnet)

- **Composition**: Y₃Al₅O₁₂ — a synthetic garnet with no natural counterpart (though the mineral yttrogarnet exists in nature only as microscopic grains).
- **Color**: Colorless (most common as diamond simulant), also produced in green, yellow, pink, blue, and red. The colors are typically very clean and even.
- **RI**: 1.833 — notably lower than diamond (2.417), so the luster and brilliance are lower than diamond. A YAG will look slightly "dull" compared to diamond.
- **SG**: 4.55 — significantly heavier than diamond (3.52). YAG feels heavy in the hand.
- **Dispersion**: 0.028 — lower than diamond (0.044). YAG does not show much fire.
- **Birefringence**: None — YAG is cubic (isotropic). Under crossed polarizers, it shows complete extinction. This distinguishes it from double-refractive simulants like synthetic rutile or CZ (which can show ADR).
- **Hardness**: 8–8.5 — good hardness for jewelry.
- **Magnification**: Very clean. Occasional metallic inclusions from the crucible or bubbles may be present, but are rare.
- **UV fluorescence**: Variable. Colorless YAG is often inert. Some doped varieties show moderate fluorescence.
- **Heat conductivity**: Low — a thermal probe will read "not diamond."
- **Detection**: The combination of moderate RI (1.833), high SG (4.55), isotropic nature, and low dispersion is diagnostic. Colorless YAG is easily identified by a refractometer reading.
- **Use**: Primarily a diamond simulant in the 1970s–80s before being largely supplanted by CZ. Also used in some colored-gem roles.
- **Trade names**: YAG, Linde YAG, Diamondite.

### GGG (Gadolinium Gallium Garnet)

- **Composition**: Gd₃Ga₅O₁₂ — another synthetic garnet with no natural counterpart.
- **Color**: Colorless to very pale yellow-green. Darker colors can be produced with dopants.
- **RI**: 1.970 — close to diamond's 2.417 but still distinctly lower. The luster is high, approaching diamond's.
- **SG**: 7.03 — extremely heavy. A GGG stone feels remarkably heavy for its size, much heavier than diamond, CZ, or YAG. This is the most immediate clue.
- **Dispersion**: 0.038 — slightly lower than diamond (0.044). Fire is moderate.
- **Isotropic**: Cubic, no birefringence, but may show weak ADR.
- **Hardness**: 6–7 — softer than CZ or YAG, and facet edges may show wear.
- **Magnification**: Gas bubbles are common and often larger than in other synthetics. Opaque white inclusions with a distinctive "puckered" or wrinkled appearance are characteristic. These are thought to be gallium oxide inclusions.
- **Fragility**: GGG cracks easily — both during growth and in jewelry. Cracked or chipped stones are frequently encountered in vintage GGG jewelry.
- **UV fluorescence**: Usually inert.
- **Detection**: The enormously high SG (7.03) is the key identifier. Combined with moderate RI (1.970), moderate dispersion, and isotropic character, GGG is not difficult to identify.
- **Use**: Diamond simulant in the 1970s–80s. Less common than YAG or CZ.
- **Trade name**: Galliant.

### Alexandrite (Czochralski)

- **Growth features**: No curved striae — unlike earlier flame-fusion alexandrite. The material is transparent and clean.
- **Inclusions**: Metallic inclusions (iridium or platinum from crucible) are the main diagnostic feature. Occasional gas bubbles may be present but are rare.
- **Color change**: Present, and can be quite attractive. The color change in Czochralski alexandrite typically goes from blue-green (daylight) to purple-red (incandescent). However, the color change may differ in tone and saturation from fine natural alexandrite — often more washed-out or with a slightly different hue balance.
- **FTIR spectroscopy**: No hydrogen absorption peaks. Czochralski growth is a "dry" method (no water in the melt), so there are no OH or water bands in the infrared spectrum. This is a critical test: natural alexandrite (and flux-grown alexandrite) typically show OH absorption bands around 2163, 2430, and 4150 cm⁻¹. Czochralski alexandrite has a flat FTIR spectrum in these regions.
- **Trace elements**: Czochralski alexandrite lacks the vanadium and chromium trace element profiles of natural alexandrite. LA-ICP-MS can definitively separate them.
- **Detection difficulty**: Hard without advanced equipment. The combination of FTIR (flat spectrum) and microscopic examination (metallic inclusions, no natural inclusions) is required for confident identification.
- **Market note**: Most synthetic alexandrite on the market today is Czochralski-grown. It is much less expensive than natural alexandrite but far more expensive than flame-fusion material.

## 4. Flux Growth

### Process Description

The flux growth method, also known as flux-melt growth, involves dissolving the nutrient materials in a molten flux (a low-melting-point solvent) and then slowly cooling the solution to precipitate crystals. This method most closely approximates the natural conditions of gem formation, producing high-quality crystals with well-formed facets and natural-looking inclusions.

The process:
- The nutrient (e.g., Al₂O₃ + Cr for ruby) is mixed with a flux (typically lead oxide PbO, molybdenum trioxide MoO₃, or boric oxide B₂O₃, often in combination)
- The mixture is heated to 1200–1400°C in a platinum crucible, forming a homogenous melt
- The melt is cooled very slowly — typically 0.5–5°C per hour over weeks or months
- As the solution becomes supersaturated, crystals nucleate and grow, either on a seed or spontaneously
- When cooling is complete, the flux is poured off or dissolved in acid, and the crystals are harvested

Key characteristics:
- Growth time: Weeks to months (very slow compared to melt methods)
- Growth temperature: 1200–1400°C (lower than melt methods but higher than hydrothermal)
- Crucible: Nearly always platinum (Pt) — sometimes with a platinum lid or baffle
- Crystal habit: Often well-formed, with natural-looking crystal faces — unlike the boules of melt methods
- Crystal size: Typically 5–50 carats, occasionally larger

Flux growth produces the most convincing synthetics because:
- The low growth temperature and slow rate reduce strain and defects
- Crystals grow as faceted individuals, not boules
- Inclusions (flux residues, platinum particles) can resemble natural features
- No curved striae — a major advantage over melt methods

### General Identification Features

**Flux inclusions** — These are the most important diagnostic feature for flux-grown gems and come in several characteristic forms:
- **Breadcrumb-like residues**: Irregular, granular, brownish or whitish particles scattered in veils or clusters. They look like tiny crumbs of bread.
- **Comma-shaped or tadpole trails**: Flux that has been trapped and stretched during growth, forming curved comma-like or tadpole-shaped features with the "tail" pointing in the growth direction.
- **Twisted veils**: Delicate, twisted, ribbon-like or veil-like patterns formed by flux trapped along growth planes. These can look like twisted silk or smoke.
- **Dust clouds**: Fine, cloud-like aggregations of tiny flux particles that resemble a dusty fingerprint.
- **Droplet-shaped flux**: Spherical or elongated droplets of solidified flux — these can look like liquid inclusions but are actually glassy.
- **Fingerprint patterns**: Flux residues often arrange in fingerprint-like patterns along healed fractures or growth planes.

**Platinum inclusions** — The platinum crucible is the source. Platinum particles appear as:
- Square or rectangular platelets (the most characteristic form)
- Hexagonal or triangular plates (less common)
- Bright metallic luster under reflected light
- Typically 10–100 microns in size
- Distributed along growth horizons or clustered in veils
- Distinguished from natural mineral inclusions by their shape (square/rectangular) and extreme metallic reflectivity

**Flat growth planes** — Flux-grown crystals often contain parallel, flat growth planes that appear as thin films or veils. These represent pauses or resumptions in growth. They are typically flat and parallel to crystal faces, unlike the curved growth lines of melt methods.

**No curved striae** — Flux growth is too slow and too well-controlled to produce curved striae. The absence of curved striae in a stone that otherwise looks synthetic (unusual color, too clean, wrong inclusions) is strongly suggestive of flux or Czochralski growth.

**Color zoning** — Flux-grown stones often show uneven, patchy, or streaky color zoning that looks different from natural color zoning. Natural color zoning typically follows crystal faces and is angular. Flux zoning can be swirling, patchy, or concentrated in irregular bands. This is particularly true for flux-grown sapphire and alexandrite.

**Occasional gas bubbles** — While uncommon, gas bubbles can occur in flux-grown material, especially if the flux composition releases gas during cooling. However, they are much rarer than in melt-method synthetics.

**UV fluorescence** — Can be similar to natural equivalents, making UV less useful than for flame-fusion material.

### Ruby (Flux)

- **Flux residues**: Breadcrumb-like residues are the most common inclusion. They often appear as whitish or brownish irregular particles in twisted veils or cloud-like patterns. Under high magnification (40–80×), the particles have a distinct granular or sugary texture that is quite different from natural inclusions.
- **Platinum platelets**: Square or rectangular metallic platelets are common. They are best seen with darkfield illumination at 40–80× magnification. They reflect light brilliantly and look like tiny mirrors embedded in the stone. No natural ruby contains square platinum platelets.
- **Color**: Excellent — the color of flux-grown ruby can rival the finest Burmese pigeon's-blood ruby. The chromium doping is precise, producing a deep, rich red without the orange tint of flame-fusion ruby. Some flux-grown ruby is so convincing that even experienced gemologists can be fooled.
- **UV fluorescence**: Strong red (LW and SW), similar to natural ruby. Not diagnostic by itself.
- **FTIR spectroscopy**: No water or OH absorption peaks. Flux growth is a "dry" method (no water in the melt). This can distinguish flux-grown ruby from some natural rubies that show OH peaks, but is not definitive on its own (some natural rubies also lack OH peaks).
- **Trade names**: Chatham ruby, Kashan ruby, Ramaura ruby, Seiko synthetic ruby (flux).
- **Chatham ruby**: The most famous flux-grown ruby. Chatham ruby was first produced in the 1950s by Carroll Chatham in San Francisco. Chatham rubies are known for their excellent color and convincing appearance. They typically contain characteristic flux veils and platinum platelets.
- **Kashan ruby**: Produced by the Kashan Corporation, these are flux-grown rubies with a distinctive slightly purplish-red color. They often show fine flux veils and occasional platinum inclusions.
- **Ramaura ruby**: A newer (1980s) flux-grown ruby with a bright red color. Ramaura rubies are distinctive for their strong red fluorescence and fine flux veils.
- **Detection checklist**: (1) Flux residues present? If yes, synthetic. (2) Platinum platelets? Synthetic. (3) No curved striae? Not Verneuil. (4) FTIR flat? Consistent with flux. (5) Too clean + too good color + too low price? Red flags.

### Sapphire (Flux)

- **Flux inclusions**: Characteristic breadcrumb-like residues and twisted veils. These can be harder to spot in blue sapphire because the deep blue color obscures them — immersion lighting is essential.
- **Color zoning**: Flux-grown sapphire often exhibits uneven, patchy, or streaky color distribution. The color concentrates in irregular bands and swirls, unlike the angular, crystal-face-related zoning of natural sapphire. The zoning in flux-grown sapphire can look almost "painted on" or smeared.
- **Platinum inclusions**: Square/rectangular metallic platelets — the same as in flux-grown ruby. These are diagnostic when found in blue sapphire.
- **Color**: Excellent deep blue (cobalt-doped), comparable to the finest Ceylon or Madagascar sapphire. Also produced in pastel colors, yellow (nickel), pink (chromium), and colorless.
- **Detection difficulty**: Hard. Flux-grown blue sapphire is one of the most difficult synthetics to identify because:
  - The blue color masks many inclusions
  - The stones are very clean
  - The color is convincing
  - No curved striae
- Requires careful immersion microscopy at high magnification (40–60×) with strong fiber-optic illumination to spot flux residues. Even then, some stones require FTIR or LA-ICP-MS for definitive identification.
- **FTIR**: Similar to flux ruby — no water/OH peaks. Not helpful if the natural reference also has no water.
- **Trace elements**: The definitive test. Flux-grown sapphire will have different Ga/Mg/Fe/Ti ratios than natural sapphire. LA-ICP-MS can identify the growth method.

### Emerald (Flux)

- **Flux inclusions**: Characteristic flux particles and veil-like patterns. The flux residues in emerald are often brownish or whitish, distributed in wispy veils or comma-like features.
- **No three-phase inclusions**: Natural emerald almost always contains three-phase inclusions (liquid + gas + solid) as a diagnostic feature (especially Colombian emerald). The complete absence of three-phase inclusions in a stone that otherwise looks like emerald is highly suspicious.
- **No jagged two-phase inclusions**: Natural emerald also commonly contains jagged two-phase inclusions (liquid + gas) along healed fractures. Flux-grown emerald does not have these.
- **FTIR spectroscopy**: This is the **definitive test**. Natural emerald (beryl) contains channel water — water molecules trapped in the open channels of the beryl crystal structure. This produces characteristic water absorption bands in the mid-infrared:
  - Type I water (oriented with H–H axis parallel to c-axis): Bands at ~3692 and ~3598 cm⁻¹ (for alkali-free emerald)
  - Type II water (oriented perpendicular to c-axis, associated with alkali ions): Bands at ~3654 and ~3607 cm⁻¹
  - All natural emeralds show water peaks — it is a defining characteristic of natural emerald
  - Flux-grown emerald does not grow from a water-based solution, so it has **no water in the channels** — the FTIR spectrum is **flat in the water region**
  - A flat FTIR spectrum between 3600 and 3700 cm⁻¹ is **diagnostic** for flux-grown synthetic emerald
- **RI**: Flux-grown emerald typically has a lower refractive index than natural emerald:
  - Flux-grown: ω = 1.560–1.566, ε = 1.562–1.568
  - Natural emerald: ω = 1.577–1.583, ε = 1.571–1.577
  - The difference (about 0.015 lower for flux) is caused by the absence of alkali ions and water in the flux material
  - An RI reading significantly below the natural range is a strong indicator of flux synthesis
- **SG**: Flux-grown emerald: 2.65–2.67 (lower than natural emerald: 2.69–2.75). The lower SG reflects the absence of water and alkalis.
- **UV fluorescence**: Usually inert, similar to many natural emeralds.
- **Trade names**: Chatham emerald (the most famous flux-grown emerald), Lennix emerald, Emeraldite.
- **Detection checklist**: (1) FTIR flat in water region? → Synthetic (flux). (2) RI below 1.570? → Suspicious. (3) SG below 2.68? → Suspicious. (4) No three-phase inclusions? → Suspicious. (5) Flux veils/particles? → Synthetic.
- **Important caveat**: Hydrothermal emerald has water in its structure (it grows from water) and will show water peaks in FTIR. So FTIR distinguishes flux emerald (no water) from natural emerald (water present) but NOT hydrothermal emerald (water present) from natural emerald. For that, you need chevron graining, nailhead spicules, and trace element analysis.

### Spinel (Flux)

- **Flux residues**: Veil-like trails and breadcrumb-like particles. The flux veils in spinel are often fine and wispy.
- **Needle inclusions**: Flux-grown spinel can contain oriented needle-like inclusions at approximately 45° to the octahedral crystal faces. These are quite different from the rutile needles (silk) found in natural corundum — they are shorter, more parallel, and at a different orientation.
- **Color**: Flux-grown spinel is produced in unusual pastel hues — soft lavenders, pale blues, gentle pinks, and delicate greens. These colors are distinct from the more saturated colors of flame-fusion spinel and from the darker, more muted colors of natural spinel.
- **ADR**: Less prominent than in flame-fusion spinel. Some flux-grown spinel shows no ADR at all, making it harder to distinguish from natural spinel on this basis.
- **RI**: 1.728 (consistent), same as flame-fusion spinel.
- **Detection difficulty**: Hard. A spinel with subtle pastel color and no obvious ADR may require FTIR or trace element analysis to separate from natural.
- **Natural vs. flux spinel**: Trace elements are the definitive test. Flux spinel has different impurity profiles (higher platinum, different Mg/Al ratios) than natural spinel.

### Alexandrite (Flux)

- **Flux inclusions**: Classic flux veils, particles, and breadcrumb residues. These are often very fine and may require high magnification (50–80×) to detect.
- **Platinum platelets**: Square and rectangular metallic inclusions from the crucible. These are diagnostic.
- **Color change**: Flux-grown alexandrite typically shows a good color change from blue-green (daylight) to purple-red (incandescent). The color change can be as strong as natural alexandrite, though the saturation may differ — often the synthetic colors are slightly more pastel or have a different hue balance.
- **FTIR spectroscopy**: This is the key test. Natural alexandrite (chrysoberyl) contains hydrogen as OH, producing characteristic absorption bands in the infrared:
  - Bands at approximately 2163, 2430, and 4150 cm⁻¹ (OH stretching and combination modes)
  - These bands are consistently present in natural alexandrite
  - Flux-grown alexandrite has **no hydrogen** — the FTIR spectrum is **flat** in these regions
  - A flat FTIR spectrum between 2000 and 4500 cm⁻¹ is diagnostic for synthetic (flux or Czochralski) alexandrite
- **Trace elements**: Natural alexandrite contains variable amounts of Cr, V, and Fe. Flux-grown alexandrite has a different trace element profile. LA-ICP-MS can definitively separate them.
- **Other chrysoberyl synthetics**: Synthetic chrysoberyl (not just alexandrite) can also be grown by flux methods. The same FTIR test applies.
- **Trade name**: Chatham alexandrite (flux).
- **Detection checklist**: (1) FTIR flat (no OH bands)? → Synthetic. (2) Platinum inclusions? → Synthetic. (3) Flux residues? → Synthetic. (4) No natural inclusions? → Suspicious. (5) Price too good? → Suspicious.

## 5. Hydrothermal Growth

### Process Description

Hydrothermal growth is the most natural-like method for producing synthetic gem crystals. It involves dissolving the nutrient material in an aqueous (water-based) solution at high temperature and pressure, then crystallizing it onto a seed under controlled conditions. This process closely mimics the natural hydrothermal processes that form many gemstones in the Earth's crust.

The process:
- An autoclave (a thick-walled steel pressure vessel, often lined with platinum, silver, or a noble metal) is filled with the nutrient material and a solvent (typically water with added mineralizers such as NaOH, Na₂CO₃, or NH₄F)
- The autoclave is sealed and heated to 300–600°C, generating pressures of 500–2000 atmospheres
- A temperature gradient is maintained: the nutrient dissolves in the hotter zone and redeposits on seed crystals in the cooler zone
- Growth proceeds over days to months, depending on the material and desired crystal size
- Seed plates (thin slices of natural or synthetic material) are used to nucleate and orient the growth
- The resulting crystal "caps" the seed plate, growing outward on both sides

Key characteristics:
- Growth temperature: 300–800°C (lower than melt or flux methods)
- Growth pressure: 500–3000 atmospheres
- Growth rate: Very slow — 0.1–1 mm per day (emerald), or up to several mm per day (quartz)
- Solvent: Water with mineralizers (alkaline for quartz, acidic/slightly basic for emerald)
- Seed: Almost always present, though often cut away in faceted stones
- Crystal quality: Very high — closely approaches natural perfection

Hydrothermal growth produces the hardest-to-identify synthetics because the water-based environment closely mimics nature, and the growth rate is slow enough to produce well-ordered crystals with natural-looking inclusions.

### General Identification

**Seed plate** — The single most diagnostic feature if visible. The seed plate is a thin (~0.5–1 mm) slice of natural or synthetic material on which the synthetic crystal grew. Under magnification:
- Appears as a flat, planar feature crossing the crystal
- Often has particles, veils, or dust-like material on its surface (the "seed plane")
- May show faint parallel lines or scratches from the seed's preparation
- In faceted stones, the cutter may have positioned the table to hide or remove the seed, but remnants are often visible near the girdle or as a faint plane in immersion
- The seed plate may be visible as a color difference (the seed may have a slightly different color or clarity than the overgrowth)

**Chevron graining** — V-shaped or chevron-shaped growth banding, most characteristic of hydrothermal emerald but also seen in other beryl varieties. The chevron pattern reflects growth on the two sides of a seed plate, with growth fronts meeting at an angle. Under darkfield or immersion:
- Appears as alternating light and dark V-shaped bands
- The bands are typically parallel to each other across the stone
- They are most visible near the seed plate and diminish toward the outer edges
- Chevron graining in emerald is pathognomonic for hydrothermal synthesis

**Nailhead spicules** — Characteristic of hydrothermal emerald. These are elongated, two-phase (liquid + gas) or three-phase inclusions that have a nail-like shape — a thin "shaft" with a flattened "head" at one or both ends. They form along growth horizons and are oriented parallel to the seed plate. Not seen in any natural emerald.

**Unusual mineral inclusions** — Hydrothermal growth from a water-based solution can produce inclusion minerals that are not found in natural gemstones of the same species. For example:
- Phenakite (Be₂SiO₄) in hydrothermal emerald — a byproduct of the growth chemistry
- Acmite (aegerine, NaFeSi₂O₆) needles in some hydrothermal emeralds
- Wollastonite in some hydrothermal corundum
- These "synthetic mineral" inclusions are characteristic of the specific chemistry of hydrothermal growth

**Weak or no twinning** — For materials that are naturally twinned, the absence of twinning is a strong indicator of synthesis. The classic example is quartz: natural amethyst almost always shows polysynthetic Brazil-law twinning (visible under crossed polarizers as alternating light and dark bands), while hydrothermal amethyst typically lacks this twinning entirely.

**FTIR spectroscopy** — Unlike flux and melt methods (which are "dry" and produce crystals with no water/OH), hydrothermal crystals contain water and OH because they grow from an aqueous solution. For emerald, hydrothermal growth produces water in the beryl channels — so the FTIR shows water peaks (making FTIR useless for distinguishing hydrothermal from natural emerald, unlike the definitive FTIR test for flux emerald). For corundum, however, the presence of water is diagnostic: natural corundum does not incorporate water, but hydrothermal corundum does.

**Trace elements** — Because the growth medium is purified chemicals, the trace element profile of hydrothermal synthetics differs from natural material. Hydrothermal gems are typically much purer (fewer trace elements) than their natural counterparts, and the ratios of elements (Ga, Fe, Ti, V, Mg) are different. LA-ICP-MS is often the only definitive test.

### Emerald (Hydrothermal)

- **Chevron graining**: The single most recognizable feature. Under darkfield or fiber-optic illumination, look for alternating light and dark V-shaped bands. The apex of the V points toward the seed plate. The bands are typically fine, closely spaced, and parallel across the stone. Chevron graining is diagnostic — no natural emerald shows this pattern.
- **Nailhead spicules**: Elongated, nail-shaped two-phase or three-phase inclusions. The "head" is a small cavity (often filled with liquid and an air bubble), and the "shaft" is a thin, elongated tube extending from it. They are oriented parallel to the seed plate. These are highly diagnostic for hydrothermal emerald.
- **Phenakite crystals**: Phenakite (Be₂SiO₄) is a common byproduct of hydrothermal emerald synthesis. It forms as small, colorless, transparent crystals with a steep pyramidal or tabular habit. Under magnification, phenakite inclusions look like tiny glassy crystals. They are not found in natural emerald.
- **Two-phase inclusions**: Many small, two-phase (liquid + gas) inclusions distributed along growth bands. Natural emerald also has two-phase inclusions, but the hydrothermal ones tend to be smaller, more numerous, and more uniformly distributed.
- **Three-phase inclusions**: Rare in hydrothermal emerald (unlike Colombian natural emerald). The presence of three-phase inclusions suggests natural origin, but their absence is not definitive.
- **Color zoning**: Angular, zigzag, or chevron-shaped color zoning. Natural emerald typically has subtle, diffuse color zoning (or none), whereas hydrothermal emerald often has sharp, well-defined color banding in angular patterns.
- **FTIR**: Shows water peaks (because hydrothermal growth is water-based). This means FTIR cannot distinguish hydrothermal emerald from natural emerald. This is the critical limitation: FTIR distinguishes flux emerald from natural, but not hydrothermal from natural.
- **Trace element analysis**: The definitive test. Hydrothermal emerald has a distinct trace element profile:
  - Lower total alkali content (Na, K, Li) than natural emerald
  - Different Cr/V ratios
  - Lower Fe content
  - Higher purity in general
  - LA-ICP-MS can identify the growth method with high confidence
- **RI**: Hydrothermal emerald has a refractive index closer to natural emerald than flux does:
  - Hydrothermal: ω = 1.570–1.578, ε = 1.564–1.572
  - Natural: ω = 1.577–1.583, ε = 1.571–1.577
  - The overlap is significant — RI is not definitive for hydrothermal vs. natural
- **SG**: Hydrothermal: 2.67–2.71 (overlaps with natural 2.69–2.75)
- **Color**: Excellent — deep green comparable to fine Colombian or Zambian emerald. Chromium + vanadium doping produces a rich, vivid green.
- **Trade names**: Biron emerald, Linde emerald (hydrothermal), Novick emerald, Tairus emerald, Regal emerald, Western side emerald. Lechleitner emerald (early hydrothermal — actually a thin layer of hydrothermal emerald on faceted natural beryl).
- **Detection checklist**: (1) Chevron graining? → Hydrothermal synthetic. (2) Nailhead spicules? → Hydrothermal synthetic. (3) Phenakite inclusions? → Hydrothermal synthetic. (4) No three-phase inclusions? → Suspicious for natural. (5) FTIR with water peaks? → Does not distinguish hydrothermal from natural. (6) Trace elements? → Definitive if available.

### Quartz (Hydrothermal) — The Most Challenging Natural/Synthetic ID Problem

**Why it is the hardest**: Hydrothermal quartz synthesis produces crystals that are chemically, structurally, and physically nearly identical to natural quartz. Quartz is composed of pure SiO₂, and the hydrothermal growth process uses water-based solutions — just like natural quartz formation. This means:
- The growth medium is the same (aqueous solution)
- The temperature and pressure ranges overlap with natural conditions
- The crystal structure is identical
- Water/OH absorption in FTIR is present in both natural and synthetic

The combination of chemical purity and hydrothermal growth means that separating natural from synthetic quartz is arguably the single most difficult problem in gemological identification.

**Growth description**: Quartz is grown hydrothermally on an industrial scale — billions of carats annually — primarily for electronics (oscillators, filters) and optics. Gem-quality material is a byproduct. The typical growth uses:
- Natural quartz as nutrient (crushed natural quartz or synthetic silica)
- Alkaline mineralizer (NaOH or Na₂CO₃ solution)
- Seed plates cut parallel to the basal plane (c-axis), or at various orientations for specific color effects
- Temperature: 350–400°C, pressure: 1000–1500 atm
- Growth rate: 0.5–3 mm per day (fast for hydrothermal)
- Crystal size: Very large — boules up to 10 kg are common

**Identification features**:

- **Seed plate**: If visible, the seed plate is a flat plane with particles, dust, or veils. The seed is typically a thin slice of natural quartz and may show natural inclusions that the overgrowth lacks. However, in faceted stones, the cutter usually positions the cut to remove the seed, so it may not be visible.

- **Chevron graining**: Rare in hydrothermal quartz, but can appear if growth conditions fluctuated. The chevron pattern is much less common than in emerald.

- **Very clean, very clear — suspiciously perfect**: Hydrothermal quartz is typically completely free of inclusions. Natural quartz often contains:
  - Fluid inclusions (liquid + gas)
  - Negative crystals (small crystal-shaped cavities)
  - Mineral inclusions (rutile needles in rutilated quartz, tourmaline, etc.)
  - Growth tubes
  - Crack-like "fingerprints" (healed fractures)
  - A completely clean, perfectly clear piece of quartz larger than a few carats is statistically unlikely in nature and should raise suspicion.

- **No twinning**: This is the single most useful diagnostic feature. Natural amethyst almost **always** exhibits Brazil-law twinning — polysynthetic twinning on a microscopic scale. Under crossed polarizers, natural amethyst shows a characteristic "tartan" or "patchwork" extinction pattern due to the alternating twin domains. Hydrothermal amethyst typically has no twinning and shows clean, uniform extinction. The presence of Brazil-law twinning strongly supports natural origin; its absence, combined with other features, supports synthetic origin. **Warning**: Some hydrothermal amethyst can be intentionally twinned by using a twinned seed, but this is uncommon.

- **FTIR spectroscopy**: Both natural and hydrothermal quartz show water/OH absorption bands:
  - Natural quartz: Broad OH absorption band around 3400 cm⁻¹, plus sharp peaks at 3585 and 3484 cm⁻¹ (related to Al-related defects)
  - Hydrothermal quartz: Similar broad OH band at 3400 cm⁻¹, but the sharp peaks may be absent or different
  - The FTIR differences are subtle and not reliable for definitive identification

- **Color origin**: Natural amethyst's purple color is caused by irradiation-induced color centers involving Fe³⁺ and Al impurities. The color is typically uneven, zoned, and variable. Hydrothermal amethyst is colored by controlled iron doping and irradiation, and the color tends to be more even and consistent. Natural citrine (yellow quartz) is rare; most commercial citrine is heat-treated amethyst (which can be natural or synthetic). Hydrothermal citrine is also produced.

- **Trace element chemistry**: Natural quartz contains trace amounts of Al, Ga, Ge, Li, Na, and K (typically tens to hundreds of ppm). Hydrothermal quartz is much purer — Al and Li levels are orders of magnitude lower. LA-ICP-MS trace element analysis:
  - Natural quartz: Al ~100–1000 ppm, Li ~1–50 ppm, Ga ~0.1–5 ppm
  - Hydrothermal quartz: Al <10–50 ppm, Li <0.1–1 ppm, Ga <0.1 ppm
  - This is the most definitive test

- **UV fluorescence**: Not helpful. Both natural and hydrothermal quartz are typically inert or show weak bluish-white fluorescence.

- **Gamma spectroscopy**: Natural quartz may show weak natural radioactivity from trace U/Th decay chains; synthetic quartz is completely free of radioactivity. This is a specialized test.

- **Market reality**: Most "amethyst" and "citrine" sold today is hydrothermal synthetic, often sold without disclosure. The price difference is small (both are inexpensive), so disclosure is less common than for expensive stones. The primary gemological concern is in the higher-end market where natural origin is claimed for value.

- **Detection difficulty**: Very hard to impossible without a seed plate, twinning observation, or trace element analysis.

### Corundum (Hydrothermal)

- **Growth features**: Hydrothermal corundum (ruby and sapphire) grows on a seed plate, typically a thin slice of natural or synthetic corundum. The seed is often visible as a flat plane with particles.
- **Color zoning**: Patchy, uneven, or irregular color distribution. The color in hydrothermal corundum tends to concentrate in irregular patches and bands that look different from the angular, crystalline zoning of natural corundum or the even color of melt-method synthetics.
- **Inclusions**: Cloudy, veil-like inclusions composed of fine particles. These are likely hydrous aluminum oxide phases or other growth byproducts. They differ from natural inclusions (silk, mineral crystals) and from flux residues.
- **FTIR spectroscopy**: This is a critical test. Natural corundum (Al₂O₃) in its pure form does not contain water in its crystal structure. However, hydrothermal corundum is grown from a water-based solution, and OH⁻ ions can substitute for O²⁻ in the lattice (charge-compensated by Mg²⁺ or other divalent cations). This produces:
  - A broad OH absorption band centered at approximately 3309 cm⁻¹ in natural corundum (from trace hydrous minerals, not from structural OH in the corundum itself)
  - Hydrothermal corundum shows a **different** OH pattern — a broad band at ~3300 cm⁻¹ with shoulders at 3100 and 3450 cm⁻¹
  - The exact band positions vary with growth conditions
  - The presence of strong OH absorption in a corundum that otherwise looks clean is suspicious
  - However, some natural corundum (especially heat-treated) can also show OH bands, so this must be interpreted carefully
- **Trace elements**: The definitive test. Hydrothermal corundum has:
  - Lower total trace element content
  - Different Ga/Mg/Fe ratios
  - No titanium (or very low Ti) in ruby
  - LA-ICP-MS can separate hydrothermal from natural with high confidence
- **Detection difficulty**: Very hard. Hydrothermal corundum is among the most convincing synthetics on the market. It requires combination microscopy (seed plate search), FTIR, and ideally trace element analysis.

### Beryl Varieties (Hydrothermal)

- **Aquamarine (blue beryl)**: Hydrothermal aquamarine is produced using iron doping. It typically shows chevron graining (though less prominent than in emerald) and occasional nailhead spicules. The color is a clean, even blue — often more uniform than natural aquamarine. FTIR shows water peaks (as it is beryl containing channel water). Trace element analysis shows higher purity and different Fe/alkali ratios.
- **Morganite (pink beryl)**: Hydrothermal morganite is manganese-doped. It typically has a very even, clean pink color. It shows similar growth features to other hydrothermal beryls (chevron graining, nailhead spicules). Natural morganite is faceted from large, often included rough — a clean, perfect morganite is suspicious.
- **Heliodor (yellow beryl)**: Produced by iron doping or irradiation. Usually shows the same growth features. Note: Most heliodor on the market is natural, but hydrothermal heliodor is increasingly common.
- **Goshenite (colorless beryl)**: Produced hydrothermally but less common. The absence of channel-water-related inclusions and the presence of growth features are the main clues.
- **General detection**: For all hydrothermal beryl varieties:
  1. Check for chevron graining (immersion, darkfield)
  2. Look for nailhead spicules
  3. Examine for seed plate remnants
  4. FTIR: Shows water peaks — does NOT distinguish from natural
  5. Trace elements: Definitive test

## 6. HPHT Diamond Synthesis

### High-Pressure High-Temperature (HPHT)

#### Growth Description

HPHT diamond synthesis, first commercialized by General Electric in 1955, reproduces the conditions under which natural diamonds form in the Earth's mantle. The process:

- Carbon source: High-purity graphite
- Metal solvent/catalyst: Typically iron (Fe), nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), or alloys thereof (e.g., Fe₇₀Ni₃₀). The metal acts both as a solvent (dissolving graphite) and as a catalyst (lowering the required pressure and temperature)
- Conditions: ~1400–1600°C and 50,000–70,000 atmospheres (5–7 GPa)
- Growth cell: A multi-anvil press (belt press, cubic press, or split-sphere press) compresses the growth capsule
- Growth: The graphite dissolves in the molten metal and migrates to the cooler seed crystal, where it precipitates as diamond
- Growth rate: ~1–10 mg/hour (much slower than gem synthetics but fast for diamond)
- Crystal size: Typical HPHT synthetic diamonds are 0.5–3 carats as-grown, occasionally larger (up to 10+ carats)
- Morphology: Cube-octahedral — the crystal form is a combination of cube {100} and octahedral {111} faces, producing a shape that differs from natural diamond's dominant octahedral form

The HPHT process has evolved significantly. Early HPHT diamonds were small, dark, and heavily included. Modern HPHT-grown diamonds (from producers like New Diamond Technology, Pure Grown Diamonds, and others) can exceed 10 carats, with colors ranging from near-colorless (Type IIa) to vivid fancy yellow (Type Ib) and blue (boron-doped).

#### Identification Features

**Metallic flux inclusions** — The single most important diagnostic feature. The metal catalyst (Fe, Ni, Co, or alloys) can become trapped in the growing crystal as:
- Dark, opaque, metallic-looking inclusions
- They appear black under transmitted light, bright metallic under reflected light
- Shapes: Irregular, rounded, or angular — often elongated along growth directions
- Size: Typically 5–100 microns, visible under 10–40× magnification
- **Magnetism**: Iron- and nickel-rich inclusions are magnetic. A strong neodymium magnet can attract some HPHT synthetics — this is a simple, non-destructive screening test. Note: Not all HPHT synthetics are magnetic (some use cobalt-rich alloys which are less magnetic).
- **X-ray fluorescence**: Flux inclusions in HPHT diamond produce characteristic XRF spectra (Fe, Ni, Co peaks). This can be detected by EDXRF.

**Cuboctahedral growth zoning** — Visible under crossed polarizers and in DiamondView imaging:
- The crystal shows distinct sector zoning corresponding to the {100} cube faces and {111} octahedral faces
- In the DiamondView, this appears as geometric sector zoning with sharp, straight boundaries between the cube and octahedral growth sectors
- The faces show different luminescence colors: typically blue or green in the {111} sectors and yellow or orange in the {100} sectors
- This sector zoning is highly characteristic — natural diamonds may show complex octahedral growth patterns but not the sharp cube-octahedral sectors

**Weak or no birefringence (strain)** under crossed polarizers:
- Natural diamonds typically show "tatami" strain patterns — cross-hatched or mosaic patterns of birefringence caused by plastic deformation in the mantle
- HPHT synthetic diamonds are grown under uniform conditions and typically show **almost no strain** — they go nearly completely dark under crossed polarizers, extinguishing uniformly
- The absence of strain is a strong indicator of HPHT synthesis
- **Warning**: Some natural diamonds (especially Type IIa) also show very low strain, so this test must be used in combination with others

**UV fluorescence**:
- Many HPHT synthetics show **strong yellow-green fluorescence** under long-wave UV, caused by aggregated nitrogen centers (H3 centers) formed during the HPHT process
- Some show **strong blue fluorescence** (especially Type IIb boron-doped blue diamonds)
- The fluorescence is often very uniform across the stone (due to the homogeneous nitrogen distribution)
- Natural diamond fluorescence is often patchy or zoned
- **Extremely strong fluorescence** (especially yellow-green) is suspicious

**Phosphorescence**:
- After exposure to short-wave UV, some HPHT synthetic diamonds phosphoresce (continue to emit light) for several seconds
- The phosphorescence is typically green, yellow-green, or blue-green
- Natural diamonds rarely show detectable phosphorescence
- Phosphorescence is a strong indicator of HPHT synthesis, especially when combined with strong yellow-green fluorescence
- The phosphorescence is linked to nitrogen-related defects (H3 centers) and boron acceptors

**Color**:
- Type IIa: Near-colorless to colorless (high-purity, no detectable nitrogen). HPHT can produce D-color diamonds that rival the finest natural gems.
- Type Ib: Yellow to golden-yellow (isolated nitrogen in the crystal lattice). The color is typically a distinctive bright, pure yellow — different from the brownish-yellow of many natural cape diamonds.
- Type IIb: Blue (boron-doped). The blue is typically a uniform, even blue — often more uniform than natural blue diamonds.
- Brown: As-grown HPHT diamonds are sometimes brown and require post-growth HPHT annealing to improve color.

**DiamondView Imaging (Most Reliable Single Test)**:

The DiamondView uses deep UV (225 nm) to excite luminescence, revealing the internal growth structure:

- **HPHT synthetic**: Geometric growth sectors with sharp, straight boundaries. The cube {100} and octahedral {111} sectors are clearly delineated. The cube sectors often luminesce yellow or orange; the octahedral sectors luminesce blue or green. The boundaries are straight and angular, forming a geometric grid-like pattern.
- **Natural diamond**: Octahedral growth layers with occasional resorption features. The growth patterns are concentric (like tree rings) rather than sectorial. Complex internal structures from multiple growth events.
- **CVD diamond**: Parallel, flat growth bands that run across the entire stone, like a deck of cards. No sector zoning.

**FTIR Spectroscopy**:

FTIR is a critical tool for typing diamond:

- **HPHT synthetic**: The most common types are:
  - **Type Ib**: Isolated substitutional nitrogen (single nitrogen atoms). This is the characteristic form of nitrogen in HPHT synthesis because the short growth time does not allow nitrogen to aggregate. The spectrum shows a sharp peak at 1130 cm⁻¹ (the Ib center) and a broad band at 1344 cm⁻¹.
  - **Type IIa**: No detectable nitrogen (for high-purity material grown with nitrogen-getters such as Al or Ti in the growth cell).
  - **Type IIb**: Boron-doped (shows boron absorption in the IR).
- **Natural diamond**: Predominantly **Type Ia** (aggregated nitrogen). The vast majority (~98%) of natural diamonds are Type Ia, containing nitrogen in aggregated forms:
  - **IaA**: Paired nitrogen atoms (A-centers), absorption at 1282 cm⁻¹
  - **IaB**: Four-nitrogen aggregates (B-centers), absorption at 1175 cm⁻¹
  - Natural Type Ib diamonds are extremely rare (only ~0.1% of natural diamonds)
  - Natural Type IIa diamonds exist but are rare (~2%)
- **HPHT treatment vs. synthesis**: FTIR can also distinguish HPHT treatment (annealing natural brown diamonds) from HPHT synthesis. HPHT treatment changes the nitrogen aggregation state — it converts Ib to Ia or creates H3 centers. HPHT synthesis preserves nitrogen as isolated (Ib) unless deliberately annealed.

### Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) Diamond

#### Growth Description

CVD diamond synthesis, developed in the 1980s and commercialized for gem-quality in the 2010s, grows diamond from a low-pressure gas plasma:

- Gas mixture: Methane (CH₄) as the carbon source, hydrogen (H₂) as the dominant gas (typically 0.5–5% CH₄ in H₂)
- Activation: Microwave plasma, hot filament, or DC arc breaks the gas molecules into reactive radicals (atomic hydrogen and methyl radicals CH₃·)
- Substrate: A flat diamond seed plate (typically a 1–10 mm² piece of HPHT synthetic diamond)
- Temperature: ~700–1000°C
- Pressure: ~1/100 atmosphere (1–50 Torr)
- Growth rate: 1–20 μm/hour (very slow — a 1-carat stone may take weeks to grow)
- Growth: Diamond grows epitaxially on the seed, building up as a flat plate

Key process characteristics:
- The seed plate is cut parallel to {100} (cubic face) to encourage step-flow growth
- The crystal grows as a **flat plate** — the final rough is wafer-thin, like a coin
- Nitrogen can be added to the gas to stabilize the growth (nitrogen converts the growth mode from {111} to {100}, producing clearer material)
- Post-growth treatment: Most CVD diamonds are HPHT-annealed after growth to:
  - Remove the brown color (which results from vacancy-related defects)
  - Improve transparency
  - Produce near-colorless (D–J) material
- Some CVD diamonds are also irradiated and annealed for fancy colors

CVD diamond technology has advanced rapidly. As of the current market, CVD diamonds up to 10+ carats are commercially available, with colors from colorless to fancy brown, pink, and yellow. CVD diamond is now the dominant method for producing high-quality synthetic diamonds (HPHT still dominates for industrial applications).

#### Identification Features

**Flat plate morphology** — The rough CVD diamond is a flat, wafer-like plate. Unlike natural diamond (which forms octahedral or dodecahedral crystals) and HPHT synthetic diamond (which forms cuboctahedral crystals), CVD diamond:
- Grows as a thin, flat plate parallel to the {100} face
- Often has a darker, graphitic edge (the edge of the growth surface)
- May have a rough, textured surface on the growth face
- If the rough is available, the flat plate morphology is diagnostic

**Strain pattern under crossed polarizers**:
- CVD diamond shows a distinctive "stripy" or "banded" strain pattern — alternating light and dark bands running parallel to the growth direction
- This is different from:
  - Natural diamond's "tatami" (cross-hatched) pattern
  - HPHT's near-complete extinction
- The banded strain is caused by slight differences in lattice parameter between growth layers
- The pattern is most visible when the stone is rotated under crossed polars

**Brown color (as-grown)** — Most as-grown CVD diamonds are brown, varying from very light brown to dark brownish. The brown color is caused by vacancy-related defects (the "H2" or "brown" defect — related to the 1.2 eV absorption band). This brown coloration is removed by post-growth HPHT annealing, which heals the vacancies. A diamond with:
- Strong brown color as-grown → CVD (or HPHT with certain treatments)
- Brown color that has been removed by HPHT → the stone shows evidence of two treatments (CVD + HPHT)
- The presence of strong brown followed by HPHT color improvement is strongly associated with CVD

**Graphite/amorphous carbon inclusions**:
- Dark, irregular, flake-like or dendritic inclusions — graphitic carbon that was not incorporated into the diamond structure
- These are typically found near the edges or substrate interface
- They appear as black irregular spots or flakes under magnification
- Not found in natural diamond (natural diamond's dark inclusions are typically sulfide or oxide minerals)

**UV fluorescence**:
- CVD diamond often shows **strong green to orange-red fluorescence** under UV
- The fluorescence is caused by NV centers (nitrogen-vacancy defects) — specifically:
  - NV⁰ (neutral nitrogen-vacancy): Zero-phonon line (ZPL) at 575 nm, produces orange-red fluorescence
  - NV⁻ (negatively charged NV): ZPL at 637 nm, produces red fluorescence
- The combination of NV centers gives a green-to-orange fluorescence
- This fluorescence can help distinguish CVD from HPHT (which tends to show more yellow-green H3 fluorescence)

**DiamondView imaging (Most Reliable)**:
- CVD diamond shows **flat, parallel growth layers** — like a stack of plywood or a deck of cards
- The layers are parallel to the seed plate and run across the entire stone
- There is no sector zoning (unlike HPHT)
- The growth layers are typically fine and closely spaced
- At the edges, the layers may curve or show step-like features
- This parallel, layered growth structure is diagnostic for CVD

**Spectroscopic Identification — The Definitive Tests**:

- **Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy**:
  - **NV centers**: NV⁰ at 575 nm and NV⁻ at 637 nm are almost always present. These are common in CVD diamond because the growth process creates many vacancies that pair with nitrogen.
  - **SiV center at 737 nm**: The silicon-vacancy center. Silicon is not a natural diamond impurity — it comes from the CVD growth chamber (the quartz windows or silica components of the reactor). The presence of a sharp SiV peak at 737 nm in PL is **very diagnostic** for CVD diamond. Natural diamonds almost never contain SiV (only very rare natural diamonds with Si inclusions, and even then the SiV signal is weak or absent).
  - **H3 center at 503 nm**: Present if the diamond has been HPHT-annealed after growth (the H3 center forms when A-centers capture a vacancy). This is common in post-treated CVD.
  - **GR1 center at 741 nm**: The neutral vacancy center. Often present in as-grown CVD (vacancies created during growth).

- **FTIR spectroscopy**:
  - **Type IIa**: High-quality CVD diamond is typically Type IIa (no detectable nitrogen). This is the most common type for gem-quality CVD.
  - **Type Ib**: Some CVD diamond uses nitrogen doping for {100} growth stabilization, producing isolated nitrogen (Ib). The FTIR shows the Ib peak at 1130 cm⁻¹.
  - **No Ia aggregation**: CVD diamond never shows Type Ia nitrogen aggregation (A-centers or B-centers) because the growth temperature is too low and the growth time too short for nitrogen to migrate and aggregate. If a diamond shows Ia aggregation, it is either natural or has been HPHT-treated.
  - **Hydrogen-related peaks**: CVD diamond commonly shows CH-related peaks at ~2920 and ~2850 cm⁻¹ (C-H stretching vibrations from methane in the growth gas). These are not typical in natural diamond.

- **Trace element analysis (LA-ICP-MS)**:
  - CVD diamond contains virtually no nitrogen (Type IIa material)
  - Silicon from the growth chamber is often detected (Si in natural diamond is usually below detection limits)
  - No aggregated nitrogen centers
  - The trace element profile is distinctly different from natural diamond

**Advanced laboratory protocol for CVD identification**:
1. DiamondView: Parallel flat growth bands? → CVD
2. PL spectroscopy: SiV at 737 nm? → CVD
3. PL spectroscopy: Strong NV centers (575/637 nm)? → CVD
4. FTIR: Type IIa or Ib with CH peaks at 2920 cm⁻¹? → CVD
5. Crossed polarizers: Stripy banded strain pattern? → CVD
6. Magnification: Graphite inclusions? → CVD

## 7. Skull Melting

### Process Description

Skull melting, also known as cold-crucible induction melting, is a specialized technique primarily used for growing cubic zirconia (CZ) crystals. The process:

- Raw material: ZrO₂ powder mixed with a stabilizer (typically CaO, MgO, or Y₂O₃ — Y₂O₃ produces the finest quality)
- Crucible: A water-cooled copper "skull" crucible — a set of copper fingers arranged in a cylinder, with water circulating through them
- Heating: A high-frequency (4–10 MHz) induction coil surrounds the crucible, inducing eddy currents in the material
- Melting: The material in the center of the crucible melts at ~2750°C. The outer layer in contact with the cooled copper fingers remains solid, forming a "skull" of unmelted material that protects the copper from the molten center
- Stabilization: Pure ZrO₂ undergoes a destructive phase transition on cooling (monoclinic ↔ tetragonal). Adding Y₂O₃ (typically 8–15 mol%) stabilizes the cubic phase at room temperature
- Growth: The crucible is slowly lowered from the coil, or the coil is raised, allowing directional solidification from the bottom
- Boule: The resulting crystal is a large cylindrical boule, typically 5–15 cm in diameter and 10–30 cm long, often with a faceted surface

Skull melting was developed in the Soviet Union in the 1970s and commercialized for gem use as "cubic zirconia" in the late 1970s. It remains the primary method for producing CZ, the most important diamond simulant.

### Cubic Zirconia (CZ)

**Physical properties**:
- **RI**: 2.15–2.18 — lower than diamond's 2.417. The lower RI means CZ has less brilliance (total internal reflection) than diamond. The difference is noticeable to the trained eye: CZ looks slightly "duller" or "glassier" than diamond.
- **Dispersion**: 0.058–0.066 — higher than diamond's 0.044. CZ shows more fire than diamond — it flashes more spectral colors. This is a useful visual clue: CZ tends to look "rainbow-y" or "glittery" in moderate light.
- **SG**: 5.6–6.0 — much heavier than diamond (3.52). A CZ stone feels notably heavier than a diamond of the same size. This is one of the simplest screening tests — a loose stone can be hefted.
- **Hardness**: 8–8.5 — softer than diamond (10). Over time, CZ will show facet-edge wear, abrasion, and surface scratches. Diamond's facet edges remain sharp.
- **Crystal system**: Cubic (isotropic). Under crossed polarizers, CZ should show complete extinction (dark in all orientations). However:
  - Some CZ shows **anomalous double refraction (ADR)** — a patchy or stripy extinction pattern
  - This is caused by internal strain from the rapid growth and cooling
  - The ADR can be confused with birefringence, but CZ is truly isotropic

**Inclusions**:
- **Gas bubbles**: CZ can contain round or elongated gas bubbles trapped during growth
- **Veils**: Wispy, veil-like inclusions caused by fluctuations in growth conditions
- **Unmelted flux particles**: Small, irregular particles of Y₂O₃ or other stabilizer that did not fully dissolve
- **Fingerprints**: Healed fracture-like patterns — but CZ does not have natural-looking mineral inclusions, crystal inclusions, or metallic inclusions

**Visual clues**:
- **"Crust" on pavilion facets**: CZ tends to have a distinctive surface texture — a slightly orange-peel-like or crust-like texture on the pavilion facets, caused by the rapid growth and the difficulty of polishing this hard material perfectly
- **Luster**: CZ has lower luster than diamond. The difference is subtle but real: diamond has the highest luster of any transparent gem (adamantine), while CZ is "sub-adamantine." The difference is most noticeable when a CZ and a diamond are compared side by side.
- **Fire vs. brilliance**: CZ has more fire (dispersion) but less brilliance (light return) than diamond. In a well-cut stone, CZ looks more colorful but less "sparkly" in white light.

**Detection summary**:
1. Thermal conductivity test: CZ does NOT conduct heat well. A diamond tester (thermal probe) will read "not diamond." This is the simplest definitive test.
2. SG heavy: CZ is 60–70% heavier than diamond. A loose stone can be identified by heft.
3. ADR under crossed polars: If the stone is isotropic but shows anomalous extinction, it may be CZ (or synthetic spinel — check RI).
4. RI: 2.15–2.18 on a refractometer (far point method, using contact liquid) is diagnostic for CZ.
5. Dispersion: Visual impression of strong fire combined with lower brilliance.
6. Magnification: Bubbles, veils, no natural inclusions, surface crust.

**CZ vs. other diamond simulants**:

| Property | Diamond | CZ | Moissanite | YAG | GGG | Strontium Titanate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RI | 2.417 | 2.15–2.18 | 2.65–2.69 | 1.833 | 1.970 | 2.409 |
| Dispersion | 0.044 | 0.058–0.066 | 0.104 | 0.028 | 0.038 | 0.190 |
| SG | 3.52 | 5.6–6.0 | 3.21–3.22 | 4.55 | 7.03 | 5.13 |
| Hardness | 10 | 8–8.5 | 9.25 | 8–8.5 | 6–7 | 5–6 |
| Thermal cond. | Very high | Low | Very high | Low | Low | Low |

- Moissanite (SiC) is the most convincing diamond simulant because its thermal conductivity is close to diamond's — it can fool thermal probes. Moissanite is doubly refractive (birefringence 0.043) and shows characteristic "fuzzy" facet edges from doubling. Its SG (3.21) is lower than diamond's, and it is typically identified by the combination of high RI, high dispersion, double refraction, and low SG.
- CZ remains the most common diamond simulant due to its low cost and good appearance.

## 9. Practical Identification Workflow

The following is a step-by-step approach for an AI-assisted identification: guiding a user through progressively more advanced checks to determine if a stone is natural or synthetic.

### Step 1: Gather Basic Information
- Ask the user about the stone: What species is it claimed to be? What color? What size? What clarity?
- Establish context: Where was it purchased? At what price? Is there a certificate?
- **Rule of thumb**: If the price seems too good for a natural stone of that size and quality, start with a high index of suspicion.

### Step 2: Clarity Assessment
- Is the stone suspiciously clean? Natural stones almost always have some inclusions — even "eye-clean" stones have features under magnification.
- A completely clean, flawless large stone is statistically unlikely to be natural.
- **Screening question**: "Under 10× magnification, do you see any crystals, needles, fingerprints, feathers, or growth lines? Or is it perfectly clean?"

### Step 3: Color Assessment
- Is the color unnaturally pure, intense, or saturated? Flame-fusion colors are often too perfect.
- Is the color too even? Natural stones (especially sapphire, amethyst, tourmaline) typically show some color variation or zoning.
- Are there colors that are unknown in nature for this species? (e.g., intense cobalt blue spinel, bright turquoise spinel, perfect colorless quartz)
- **Screening question**: "Does the color look completely even, or do you see patches, bands, or zones of different color intensity?"

### Step 4: Refractive Index (RI) Check
- Measure the RI with a refractometer. Is it within the expected range for the species?
- If the stone is claimed to be emerald:
  - RI below 1.570 → strong indicator of flux synthesis
  - RI 1.577–1.583 → consistent with natural or hydrothermal
- If the stone is claimed to be spinel:
  - RI exactly 1.728 → suspicious for synthetic (especially if combined with ADR)
- If the stone is a diamond simulant:
  - CZ: RI ~2.15–2.18
  - YAG: RI 1.833
  - GGG: RI 1.970
  - Strontium titanate: RI 2.409
  - Moissanite: RI 2.65–2.69 (double refractive)

### Step 5: Specific Gravity (SG) Measurement
- Hydrostatic weighing gives the SG. Compare to the expected natural range.
- Diamond simulants: CZ (5.6–6.0), YAG (4.55), GGG (7.03), ST (5.13) are all much heavier than diamond (3.52)
- **Screening question**: "Does the stone feel heavy or light for its size?"

### Step 6: Magnification — Systematic Search
Use a microscope at 10–40× with darkfield and/or fiber-optic illumination. Search systematically:

- **Curved striae**: Run your focus across the stone. If you see curved parallel bands, the stone is Verneuil synthetic. Most diagnostic for ruby, sapphire.
- **Gas bubbles**: Round, elongated, or tadpole-shaped cavities. Common in flame fusion and skull melt.
- **Flux residues**: Breadcrumb-like particles, twisted veils, dust clouds, comma-shaped trails. Look in veil patterns and along growth planes. Diagnostic for flux-grown stones.
- **Platinum inclusions**: Square/rectangular metallic platelets. Bright reflective. Diagnostic for flux or Czochralski growth.
- **Metallic inclusions (diamond)**: Dark opaque metallic specks (Fe/Ni/Co). Check with reflected light for metallic luster. Diagnostic for HPHT diamond.
- **Seed plate**: A flat plane crossing the stone, often with particles or veils on it. Diagnostic for hydrothermal growth.
- **Chevron graining**: V-shaped growth bands. Diagnostic for hydrothermal emerald.
- **Nailhead spicules**: Nail-shaped two-phase inclusions. Diagnostic for hydrothermal emerald.
- **Three-phase inclusions**: If found in emerald, strongly supports natural (especially Colombian). If completely absent, consider flux or hydrothermal.
- **Natural mineral inclusions**: Crystals, rutile needles ("silk"), apatite, calcite, etc. — these support natural origin but their absence is not diagnostic of synthetic.

### Step 7: UV Fluorescence
- **Ruby**: Very strong red (both LW and SW) is characteristic of flame-fusion synthetic. Natural ruby often shows weaker or more variable fluorescence.
- **Sapphire**: Chalky bluish-white under LW is suspicious for synthetic.
- **Diamond**: Yellow-green fluorescence (especially with phosphorescence) strongly suggests HPHT synthetic. Blue fluorescence is typical of many natural diamonds.
- **Spinel**: Inert under UV suggests synthetic (many natural spinels show red fluorescence).

### Step 8: Crossed Polarizers (Conoscopy)
- **Isotropic stones**: Should show complete extinction. Anomalous double refraction (patchy extinction) → likely synthetic spinel (flame fusion) or CZ.
- **Anisotropic stones**: 
  - Look for strain patterns. Natural diamond shows "tatami" or cross-hatched strain. HPHT diamond shows near-complete extinction (no strain). CVD diamond shows stripy banded strain.
  - Quartz: Look for Brazil-law twinning (tartan pattern in amethyst). If no twinning and the stone is amethyst, consider hydrothermal synthetic.

### Step 9: Chelsea Colour Filter (CCF)
- **Ruby**: Very strong bright red under CCF → synthetic (flame fusion). Natural ruby also shows red under CCF, but usually less intense.
- **Sapphire**: Pale greenish under CCF (natural); bright red or pink under CCF → synthetic cobalt-doped sapphire.
- **Emerald**: Natural emerald shows pinkish-red under CCF (Colombian) or green (other origins). Synthetic emerald (most) shows strong red under CCF.
- **Spinel**: Pale reddish under CCF (natural); strong red under CCF → synthetic cobalt-blue spinel.

### Step 10: Spectroscope (Hand-Held)
- Check for natural absorption features:
  - **Ruby**: Chrome lines (fine lines in the red). Both natural and synthetic show these — not diagnostic.
  - **Sapphire**: Iron lines at 450, 460, 470 nm in natural blue sapphire. Synthetic blue sapphire (cobalt-doped) shows broad absorption in the yellow-orange region instead.
  - **Emerald**: Chrome lines in the red (both natural and synthetic). Not diagnostic.
  - **Diamond**: The Cap (430 nm line) in Type Ia natural diamond. Type IIa diamonds (natural or synthetic) lack this line.
  - **Spinel**: Natural red spinel shows chrome spectrum (similar to ruby). Synthetic cobalt-blue spinel shows a characteristic cobalt spectrum with absorption bands in the yellow, green, and blue.

### Step 11: Advanced Laboratory Testing
If the stone is still not identified after the above steps, the following laboratory techniques are required:

- **FTIR (Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy)**:
  - Emerald: Determines the presence or absence of water in the beryl channels. No water → flux synthetic. Water present → natural or hydrothermal.
  - Alexandrite: Checks for OH bands (2163, 2430, 4150 cm⁻¹). No OH → synthetic (flux or Czochralski).
  - Diamond: Determines diamond type (Ia, Ib, IIa, IIb). HPHT synthetic is usually Ib or IIa. CVD is usually IIa. Natural is usually Ia.
  - Corundum: Checks for OH bands — hydrothermal corundum shows water bands that are absent in natural, melt, and flux corundum.

- **LA-ICP-MS (Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry)**:
  - Provides quantitative trace element analysis
  - The definitive test for most natural vs. synthetic separations
  - Compares element ratios (Ga/Mg, Ga/Fe, Cr/V, Fe/Ti, etc.) against reference data for natural reference populations
  - Can identify the geographic origin of natural stones in addition to separating natural from synthetic

- **PL (Photoluminescence) Spectroscopy**:
  - Diamond: Detects NV centers (575, 637 nm), SiV (737 nm — diagnostic for CVD), H3 (503 nm), GR1 (741 nm)
  - Corundum: Detects Cr³⁺ lines, but natural and synthetic can be similar
  - A critical tool for diamond identification

- **DiamondView (Deep UV imaging)**:
  - The most reliable single test for diamond synthesis
  - Visualizes growth structures: sector zoning (HPHT), parallel layers (CVD), octahedral patterns (natural)
  - Should be combined with PL and FTIR for definitive identification

- **Raman Spectroscopy**:
  - Identifies inclusion minerals (e.g., phenakite in hydrothermal emerald)
  - Measures residual stress (via Raman peak shift)
  - Less commonly used for natural/synthetic identification but can be helpful for inclusion characterization

### Decision Tree for Common Scenarios

**Scenario A: "Is this ruby natural?"**
1. Magnification: Curved striae? → Yes: Flame fusion synthetic.
2. No curved striae. Flux residues or Pt platelets? → Yes: Flux synthetic.
3. Not obviously flux or Verneuil. Seed plate or patchy zoning? → Possibly hydrothermal.
4. Looks clean with uniform color. → Could be Czochralski.
5. Has natural-looking mineral inclusions? → Likely natural (but not guaranteed — some synthetics mimic natural inclusions).
6. FTIR: OH bands? → Natural or hydrothermal. No OH? → Flux or Czochralski.
7. LA-ICP-MS: Definitive answer.

**Scenario B: "Is this emerald natural or synthetic?"**
1. FTIR: Water peaks absent? → Flux synthetic. Water peaks present? → Natural or hydrothermal. (This is the most important single question.)
2. Magnification: Chevron graining? → Hydrothermal synthetic.
3. Magnification: Nailhead spicules? → Hydrothermal synthetic.
4. Magnification: Three-phase inclusions? → Likely natural (Colombian).
5. Magnification: Flux veils or breadcrumbs? → Flux synthetic.
6. RI: Below 1.570? → Flux synthetic.
7. Trace elements: Definitive answer between natural and hydrothermal.

**Scenario C: "Is this diamond natural?"**
1. Magnification: Metallic inclusions? → HPHT synthetic. Graphite flakes? → CVD.
2. DiamondView: Geometric sector zones? → HPHT. Flat parallel bands? → CVD. Octahedral patterns? → Natural.
3. PL: SiV at 737 nm? → CVD.
4. FTIR: Type Ia (aggregated nitrogen)? → Natural. Type Ib or IIa? → Could be synthetic (or rare natural).
5. Crossed polars: No strain? → HPHT synthetic. Stripy strain? → CVD. Tatami strain? → Natural.
6. UV fluorescence: Strong yellow-green with phosphorescence? → HPHT synthetic.

### Final Caveats

- **No single test is definitive**: Every identification should be based on the convergence of multiple tests. Exceptions exist for every rule.
- **New methods evolve**: As synthetic technology improves, new identification challenges emerge. Stay current with gemological literature.
- **Disclosure is key**: Synthetic gems are not "fakes" — they are real gemstones that should be disclosed as laboratory-grown. The gemologist's job is accurate identification, not value judgment.
- **When in doubt, send to a lab**: If a stone's origin affects its value significantly (e.g., a large ruby, a colored diamond, a fine alexandrite), and the identification is uncertain, refer the stone to a major gemological laboratory (GIA, SSEF, Gubelin, AGL, IGI, etc.) for advanced testing.
